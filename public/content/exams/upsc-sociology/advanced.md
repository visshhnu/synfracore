# UPSC Sociology Optional — Advanced (Paper II: Indian Society — Structure and Change)

Paper II applies Paper I's theory and thinkers specifically to India. UPSC's official syllabus organises it into three lettered sections (A, B, C) rather than the flat 10-unit list Paper I uses — Section A introduces the subject, Section B covers structure, Section C covers change. The question paper itself still uses the standard 8-question/5-answered format across its two halves.

## The full Paper II syllabus


## How the three sections connect

Section A is the orientation layer — it names the three big lenses through which Indian society itself has historically been studied (Ghurye's Indology, which reads Indian society through its textual/Sanskritic tradition; Srinivas's structural-functionalism, which reads it through field-based village studies and concepts like Sanskritisation; and A.R. Desai's Marxist sociology, which reads it through colonial and class exploitation). Section B is the structural inventory — caste, tribe, class, kinship, religion, and rural/agrarian organisation as they actually exist. Section C is the change layer — every mechanism (planning, land reform, industrialisation, social movements, population shifts) that has acted on Section B's structures since Independence, plus the resulting unresolved tensions.

## Why Section A's three perspectives matter more than they look

Section A is short on paper but functions as the interpretive key for the rest of Paper II. Nearly any Section B or C topic can be answered "through the lens of" Ghurye, Srinivas, or Desai, and naming which lens you're using is exactly the kind of move that separates a strong answer from a merely descriptive one. For example, caste (Section B) can be read as a textual/Brahminical institution (Ghurye's Indological approach), as a locally variable, empirically observed system organised around a "dominant caste" (Srinivas's field-based structural-functionalism), or as a system that colonialism itself hardened and rigidified for administrative convenience (Desai's Marxist-historical approach) — three different, citable answers to the same question.

## How to actually study Section B (caste in particular)

Caste is the single most frequently and variously tested topic in all of Paper II. Learn it with four named perspectives side by side — Ghurye (textual, all-India, hierarchy rooted in varna), Srinivas (field-based, locally variable, "dominant caste" and Sanskritisation as mechanisms of local status mobility), Dumont (caste as fundamentally about the ideological opposition of purity and pollution, hierarchy as the organising principle over power), and Beteille (caste, class, and power as increasingly separable dimensions in modern India, drawing on his own Tanjore village fieldwork) — plus one contemporary angle (caste's persistence in urban, political, and matrimonial-market contexts despite declining ritual salience). A question on caste rewards naming at least two of these four thinkers in contrast with each other, not just describing "the caste system" generically.

## Annotated example: a Paper II-style answer

**Question style:** "Critically examine the role of the Green Revolution in transforming India's rural class structure." (Section C: Rural and agrarian transformation)

1. **Introduction:** Define the Green Revolution (HYV seeds, assured irrigation, fertilizer package, late 1960s onward) and state that its social effects were uneven, not uniform.
2. **Body — structural starting point:** Name the pre-existing agrarian class structure it acted on (Section B: landowning cultivators, tenant farmers, landless agricultural labour).
3. **Body — the transformation:** Middle and rich peasants with assured irrigation and capital to invest (concentrated in Punjab, Haryana, western UP) captured most of the gains, some rising into a new commercially oriented "bullock capitalist" class; landless labourers saw wage gains in some regions but also displacement through mechanisation in others.
4. **Critical angle:** Present both sides — modernisation theorists credit it with breaking food scarcity and enabling upward mobility for some middle castes; a Marxist-influenced reading (echoing Desai's approach from Section A) highlights how it deepened regional and intra-village inequality rather than resolving it.
5. **Conclusion:** A reasoned judgment naming the ongoing "second Green Revolution"/crop diversification debate as the contemporary extension of this same structural tension.

This structure → mechanism of change → effect (uneven) → theoretical contrast → judgment pattern is the Paper II house style: description alone, without naming the structural starting point and citing at least one named perspective, reads as a GS1 answer, not a Sociology optional answer.

## Try it (2 minutes)

Pick one social movement you know something about (a farmers' movement, a women's movement, a Dalit movement, an environmental movement — Section C, Unit 5 lists all four categories). In two minutes, name one structural condition (from Section B) the movement was reacting against, and one specific outcome or change it produced. This structure-then-change pairing is the exact analytical move Section C questions expect.

## Study resources
- **Ram Ahuja, "Society in India"** and **"Indian Social System"** — the standard Paper II reference texts used by most serious aspirants.
- **M.N. Srinivas, "Social Change in Modern India"** and **"The Remembered Village"** — essential for the structural-functionalist perspective and village-study tradition cited throughout Section B.
- **André Béteille, "Caste, Class and Power"** — the standard reference for the caste-class-power distinction (Section B, Unit 2).
- **Yogendra Singh, "Modernization of Indian Tradition"** — widely cited for Section A/C questions on colonial impact and visions of social change.
