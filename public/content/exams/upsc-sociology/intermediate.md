# UPSC Sociology Optional — Intermediate (Paper I: Fundamentals of Sociology)

Paper I is a single continuous sequence of 10 units — universal sociological theory and method, not yet applied to India specifically. This is the exact structure and topic list as given in UPSC's official syllabus.

## The full Paper I syllabus

```
1. SOCIOLOGY – THE DISCIPLINE:
   Modernity and social changes in Europe and emergence of Sociology
   Scope of the subject and comparison with other social sciences
   Sociology and common sense

2. SOCIOLOGY AS SCIENCE:
   Science, scientific method and critique
   Major theoretical strands of research methodology
   Positivism and its critique
   Fact value and objectivity
   Non-positivist methodologies

3. RESEARCH METHODS AND ANALYSIS:
   Qualitative and quantitative methods
   Techniques of data collection
   Variables, sampling, hypothesis, reliability and validity

4. SOCIOLOGICAL THINKERS:
   Karl Marx – Historical materialism, mode of production, alienation, class struggle
   Emile Durkheim – Division of labour, social fact, suicide, religion and society
   Max Weber – Social action, ideal types, authority, bureaucracy, Protestant ethic and capitalism
   Talcott Parsons – Social system, pattern variables
   Robert K. Merton – Latent and manifest functions, conformity and deviance, reference groups
   Mead – Self and identity

5. STRATIFICATION AND MOBILITY:
   Concepts: equality, inequality, hierarchy, exclusion, poverty and deprivation
   Theories of social stratification: Structural functionalist, Marxist, Weberian
   Dimensions: class, status groups, gender, ethnicity and race
   Social mobility: open and closed systems, types, sources, and causes of mobility

6. WORKS AND ECONOMIC LIFE:
   Social organization of work in different types of society: slave society,
     feudal society, industrial/capitalist society
   Formal and informal organization of work
   Labour and society

7. POLITICS AND SOCIETY:
   Sociological theories of power
   Power elite, bureaucracy, pressure groups, and political parties
   Nation, state, citizenship, democracy, civil society, ideology
   Protest, agitation, social movements, collective action, revolution

8. RELIGION AND SOCIETY:
   Sociological theories of religion
   Types of religious practices: animism, monism, pluralism, sects, cults
   Religion in modern society: science, secularization, religious revivalism,
     fundamentalism

9. SYSTEMS OF KINSHIP:
   Family, household, marriage
   Types and forms of family
   Lineage and descent
   Patriarchy and sexual division of labour
   Contemporary trends

10. SOCIAL CHANGE IN MODERN SOCIETY:
   Sociological theories of social change
   Development and dependency
   Agents of social change
   Education and social change
   Science, technology and social change
```

## How the ten units connect

Units 1-3 (Sociology — The Discipline, Sociology as Science, Research Methods) are the methodological foundation: what the subject is, how it claims to know things, and the technical vocabulary used to describe that knowledge. Unit 4 (Sociological Thinkers) hands you the six named lenses — Marx, Durkheim, Weber, Parsons, Merton, and Mead — that every later unit implicitly applies. Units 5-9 (Stratification, Work, Politics, Religion, Kinship) are substantive domains where those lenses get used on specific institutions. Unit 10 (Social Change) is the synthesis unit that ties the whole paper together, since every domain in Units 5-9 is also, eventually, asked as a "how has X changed" question.

**High-yield linkage to memorise:** Unit 4's thinkers (especially Durkheim's functionalism, Marx's conflict theory, and Weber's interpretive approach) are the three lenses re-used across Units 5-9 — stratification is explained through all three (functionalist: stratification motivates people to fill important roles; Marxist: stratification is class exploitation; Weberian: stratification has three independent dimensions — class, status, party). Learning the three-lens structure once in Unit 4 and then recognising it recur in Units 5, 7, and 8 turns five units of separate memorisation into one repeated pattern.

### How to actually study Unit 4 (Sociological Thinkers)

This is the single highest-leverage unit in Paper I because nearly every other unit borrows its vocabulary. For each of the six thinkers, build a one-page note with: (a) the core problem they were trying to solve, (b) their key named concepts, (c) one criticism of their approach, (d) one place elsewhere in the syllabus (Paper I or Paper II) where their framework gets applied again. Treating the thinkers as reusable tools, not as a closed unit to memorise once, is what separates strong Paper I and Paper II answers.

## Cross-cutting exam skill: naming a thinker or concept in every answer

Unlike Geography, Sociology has no compulsory map question, but it has an equivalent discipline: every strong answer names at least one specific thinker, concept, or Indian sociological study rather than describing a trend in plain prose. Build this habit in Paper I first — for every unit, keep a running list of "which named thinker/concept would I cite if asked about this" — because Paper II expects the exact same discipline applied to Indian material.

## Study resources
- **Vidya Bhushan & D.R. Sachdeva, "An Introduction to Sociology"** — standard first read for thinkers and basic concepts (Units 1-4).
- **C.N. Shankar Rao, "Sociology: Principles of Sociology with an Introduction to Social Thought"** — widely used for stratification, work, politics, religion, and kinship units.
- **Anthony Giddens, "Sociology" (any edition)** — a common cross-reference for the Western theoretical grounding behind Units 4, 5, and 10.
- **Previous Year Question papers (upsc.gov.in)** — the single most reliable guide to which sub-topics within each unit are actually tested most often.
