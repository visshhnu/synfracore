# Reasoning Ability — Advanced

## Mains-level puzzles: resolving contradictory-seeming clues through elimination

```
A Mains-level puzzle with 5-7 variables often includes clues
  that appear contradictory or insufficient when read in
  isolation, but resolve correctly once combined with elimination
  across the FULL clue set — this is a deliberate difficulty
  escalation from Prelims, not a sign of a flawed question

Elimination technique when direct placement stalls:
  1. List all remaining valid possibilities for the currently
     stuck variable (not just the two most obvious options)
  2. For EACH remaining possibility, check if it creates a
     contradiction with any OTHER already-placed clue
  3. Eliminate possibilities that create contradictions —
     often only one possibility survives elimination, even
     when no single clue directly determined it
```
This is the key skill differentiating Mains-level puzzle performance from Prelims-level: Prelims puzzles are generally solvable through direct, sequential clue application (the Fundamentals tab's method suffices), while Mains puzzles frequently require this elimination-across-the-full-clue-set technique specifically because no single clue directly resolves the stuck variable — only the combination of all clues, checked systematically against each remaining possibility, narrows to one valid answer. Recognizing when direct placement has stalled (rather than continuing to re-read the same clues hoping for a new insight) and switching to systematic elimination is itself a trained skill worth practicing deliberately on genuinely hard puzzles.

## Speed under Mains-level difficulty: a different time-budget mindset than Prelims

```
Prelims puzzle time budget: 2-3 minutes for a straightforward
  4-5 variable puzzle, since the direct-application method
  resolves it quickly once the most definite clue is found

Mains puzzle time budget: genuinely longer per puzzle (5-8
  minutes reasonable for a 6-7 variable puzzle with elimination
  required) — attempting to force Mains puzzles into a Prelims-
  level time budget produces either rushed, error-prone
  placement or an incomplete arrangement submitted with guessed
  gaps
```
A candidate who's built strong Prelims-level speed sometimes carries an unrealistic expectation into Mains preparation, assuming similar per-puzzle time should be achievable — Mains puzzles are deliberately harder and genuinely require more time even for a well-prepared candidate using correct technique, and the actual skill to build for Mains isn't "solve as fast as Prelims" but rather "correctly identify, within the first 60-90 seconds, whether a puzzle is solvable within a reasonable time budget or should be deprioritized in favor of other questions" — since Mains sections have limited total time across many questions, and getting stuck on one very hard puzzle at the cost of several easier questions elsewhere is a real, common strategic error.

## Computer Aptitude: a genuinely different skill category within the reasoning umbrella

```
Common Computer Aptitude topics (in banking exams specifically
  including this sub-section):
  - Number systems (binary/decimal/hex conversion)
  - Basic computer architecture/terminology
  - Networking fundamentals (basic concepts, not deep technical)
  - MS Office functional knowledge

This is NOT logical-reasoning-pattern-based like puzzles/
  syllogism — it's closer to direct factual/conceptual recall,
  requiring dedicated content study rather than pattern-
  recognition-building practice
```
Treating Computer Aptitude with the same "practice daily for pattern recognition" approach that works for puzzles is a category error — this sub-section rewards direct content knowledge (knowing binary conversion methods, basic computer terminology) more than logical pattern recognition, and candidates who apply reasoning-section study habits (heavy repetition-based puzzle practice) to Computer Aptitude without also doing direct content study of the underlying factual material often underperform in this specific sub-section relative to their general reasoning strength, simply because it's testing a genuinely different skill.

## Building genuine adaptability for novel puzzle structures

```
The realistic expectation for exam day: some puzzle structure
  will appear that doesn't exactly match any previously-practiced
  pattern — genuinely novel combinations of constraint types
  are common in well-designed exams specifically to test actual
  reasoning ability, not just memorized pattern recognition

The durable skill: the underlying METHOD (most definite clue
  first, incremental building with marked uncertainty, systematic
  elimination when stalled, final full re-verification) transfers
  to a genuinely novel puzzle structure, even one that doesn't
  match any specific pattern from practice — this is exactly why
  the method itself, not memorized pattern-matching, is the
  actual skill being built through extensive practice
```
This is the same underlying principle as durable skill-building covered elsewhere in this platform's exam-prep content (medical terminology's decoding method, for instance) — the goal of extensive puzzle practice isn't accumulating a library of memorized puzzle-type solutions to pattern-match against on exam day, it's internalizing the underlying method deeply enough that it reliably applies to a puzzle structure never seen before, which is exactly the scenario a well-designed Mains-level exam is specifically constructed to test.

## Realistic accuracy-speed tradeoff at the Mains level

```
At Mains difficulty, attempting every puzzle within a section
  is often NOT the optimal strategy — a candidate correctly
  solving 6 of 9 puzzles with high accuracy typically scores
  better than one attempting all 9 with lower accuracy due to
  rushed, error-prone placement under time pressure

The skill: quickly assessing (within the first minute) whether
  a specific puzzle's clue structure looks tractable within a
  reasonable time budget, and strategically skipping/deferring
  genuinely intractable-looking puzzles in favor of ensuring
  high accuracy on the puzzles actually attempted
```
This connects directly to the accuracy-vs-attempt-rate tradeoff framework covered in this platform's other exam-prep content — at Mains-level puzzle difficulty specifically, the strategic skill of selective attempting (rather than attempting everything indiscriminately) becomes genuinely important, not just a defensive fallback for weaker candidates, since even strong candidates lose more marks from rushed errors on over-ambitious attempts than from a well-chosen selective strategy that prioritizes accuracy on a smaller, well-chosen subset of puzzles.
