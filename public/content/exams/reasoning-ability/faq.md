# Reasoning Ability — FAQ

**Do I need to be naturally good at logical thinking to score well in Reasoning Ability, or is it genuinely trainable?**
It's genuinely trainable, and preparation quality matters more than raw intelligence specifically in this section — pattern recognition built through consistent, repeated practice is what actually drives speed and accuracy, not innate logical talent. A candidate with average natural aptitude who practices consistently using the correct methods (starting from definite clues, drawing family trees and direction-sense sketches, systematic elimination for hard puzzles) reliably outperforms one relying on natural ability without deliberate, structured practice.

**Why can't I just solve blood relations and direction sense problems mentally if I'm confident I can track them?**
Because working memory has real, measurable limits under time pressure that confidence doesn't overcome — a single misremembered relation or miscounted turn direction cascades into every subsequent step being wrong, and this error often goes unnoticed until the final answer is already incorrect. Drawing the tree or sketching the path isn't a sign of weaker ability; it's the reliable method that removes this specific, well-documented failure mode entirely, used by strong candidates as a standard practice, not just a fallback for those who struggle.

**Should I focus on Prelims-level puzzle difficulty first, or jump straight to Mains-level 6-7 variable puzzles?**
Build the core method (most-definite-clue-first, incremental building, re-verification) on Prelims-level difficulty first, since Mains-level puzzles require the same foundational method plus an additional skill (systematic elimination across the full clue set when direct placement stalls). Attempting Mains-level difficulty before the foundational method is solid tends to produce frustration without genuine skill-building, since the added complexity obscures whether errors come from the base method or the additional elimination technique.

**How much should I worry about coding-decoding patterns changing from year to year?**
It's worth genuine, specific attention — unlike most reasoning topics where the underlying method stays constant, coding-decoding pattern styles do meaningfully shift across exam years as new variations are introduced. Practicing only older, simpler fixed-shift patterns can leave a candidate caught off guard by a currently-common pattern type never encountered before, even with strong general reasoning ability — this is one of the few areas where staying current with recent-year practice specifically has direct, measurable value beyond general skill-building.

**Is it better to attempt every puzzle in a section, or skip the ones that look hard?**
At Mains-level difficulty specifically, selective attempting is often the better strategy — a candidate correctly solving fewer puzzles with high accuracy typically outscores one attempting everything with rushed, error-prone placement. The skill worth building is quickly assessing, within the first minute or so, whether a puzzle's clue structure looks tractable within a reasonable time budget, and deliberately deferring genuinely intractable-looking ones rather than getting stuck and losing time that could go toward other, more solvable questions.

**Why does my error log not seem to be improving my accuracy even though I maintain it consistently?**
An error log only drives real improvement if it's reviewed AS A SET, looking for recurring patterns across multiple entries — logging each mistake independently without ever cross-referencing past entries misses the same specific, fixable habit (a consistent counting convention error, for instance) recurring across several puzzles. Simply recording "got this wrong" isn't enough — recording specifically what went wrong and periodically reviewing the full log for repeated patterns is what turns the log into something that actually changes future performance.
