# UPSC History Optional — Scoring & What Separates Good from Average

There is no external "certification" for a UPSC optional subject — you don't get a credential for studying History the way you would for a technical certification exam. What matters instead is exactly how UPSC scores this optional and what measurably different behaviour separates candidates who score well (typically 300+/500 combined) from those who score average-to-poor (typically low-to-mid 200s/500), even when both groups know a similar volume of content.

## How the optional is actually scored

- Two papers, 250 marks each, 500 marks total — folded directly into the overall Mains merit ranking alongside the four GS papers and the Essay paper.
- Each paper: 8 questions across two sections, 5 to be attempted, Question 1 and Question 5 compulsory, at least one attempt from each section `(needs verification — recheck the exact current-year question format/compulsory-question rule against the latest official notification, since this detail can shift year to year independent of the syllabus)`.
- There is no negative marking on Mains descriptive papers — an incomplete or partially correct answer still earns partial credit, so leaving a question blank is close to the worst option, worse than attempting it imperfectly.
- Examiners mark holistically against a model answer/scheme, but with real weight given to naming the correct source, date, or historian, factual accuracy, and evidence of taking and defending an analytical position (not just narrating events) — this qualitative emphasis (not a hard numeric formula) is well-documented across aspirant/coaching post-result analyses `(needs verification — the general shape of this is well established but exact marking-scheme weightage is not publicly quantified by UPSC and should be treated as informed pattern, not official rule)`.

## What separates a good scorer from an average one

### 1. Naming a source, date, or historian in nearly every paragraph
Average scorers narrate a historical event in plain, textbook-summary language — essentially a longer GS1 answer. Good scorers reach for a specific source, precise date, or named historian's position *by default* whenever a topic has any evidentiary or interpretive dimension — citing Ranajit Guha on the Permanent Settlement, naming the exact year of the Battle of Buxar, or contrasting the colonial and nationalist readings of 1857. This is the single most consistently cited differentiator among strong History optional scorers, because it is what actually distinguishes an "optional subject" answer from a general-knowledge answer on the same topic.

### 2. Taking and defending a position, not just listing perspectives
"The colonial view says X, the nationalist view says Y" scores lower than showing *why* they disagree and reaching a reasoned judgment about which reading the evidence better supports (or where both are partially right). Average answers stop at listing interpretations side by side; good answers use the contrast itself as the analytical payload, then commit to a defensible conclusion rather than leaving the question open.

### 3. Precise dates and named events over generic time markers
A theory or trend explained purely in the abstract ("British economic policy harmed India") scores lower than the same claim explained with specific, named evidence (the Permanent Settlement of 1793, the drain of wealth estimated by Dadabhai Naoroji, the decline of the Dhaka muslin industry). Good scorers keep a running file of precise dates and named instances specifically to plug into argument-heavy answers.

### 4. Time discipline across all five questions
Because there's no negative marking, a common average-scorer mistake is over-investing in the first two questions (often the ones they feel most prepared for, typically a favourite dynasty or movement) and rushing or leaving incomplete the last one. Good scorers allocate time strictly by marks (roughly 1 minute per mark) and protect enough time to attempt all five questions with at least a basic source-and-date-anchored structure, rather than writing three excellent answers and one blank.

### 5. Balanced command across Paper I, Modern India, and World History
Because Paper II splits between Modern India (Units 1-15) and World History (Units 16-27), candidates who neglect World History to "cram" Indian history tend to be forced into a narrow, low-choice subset of Paper II questions on exam day. Good scorers maintain working command of all three blocks (Paper I, Modern India, World History) so they can select the 5 strongest questions from the full 8 offered, rather than being constrained to whichever 3-4 happen to fall in their comfort zone.
