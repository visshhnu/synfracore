# English Language — Previous Year Question Patterns

Representative question patterns reflecting recurring banking/SSC English Language question types, illustrating the applied techniques covered across this section.

**Q1 (Error detection pattern — collective noun trap).** "The committee HAVE decided to postpone the meeting until further notice." Identify the error and correct it.
The error is "HAVE" — "committee" is a collective noun functioning as a single unit here (a common exam pattern), taking the singular verb "HAS" in most standard exam-English contexts, despite "committee" referring to multiple people. This directly illustrates the "true subject vs. nearby plural sense" trap — the collective noun's plural implication tempts a plural verb, but standard grammar treats it as singular when acting as one unit.

**Q2 (Vocabulary-in-context pattern — multiple senses).** "The negotiations reached an impasse, with neither party willing to concede ground." What does "impasse" mean in this context, and how would you verify your answer choice?
"Impasse" means a deadlock or standstill where no progress can be made — verify by re-substituting: "the negotiations reached a [deadlock/standstill]" fits coherently with "neither party willing to concede," confirming the negotiations are stuck, not progressing. A candidate should check that whichever option they select for "impasse" produces this same coherent sense when substituted back into the full sentence, rather than selecting based on a vaguely-recalled definition alone.

**Q3 (Para jumble pattern — anchor identification).** Given: (A) "This shift has significant implications for urban planning." (B) "As a result, cities worldwide are seeing declining central business district occupancy." (C) "Remote work has fundamentally altered commuting patterns since 2020." (D) "Planners must now reconsider zoning models built around traditional office districts." Identify the anchor sentence and the correct order.
(C) is the anchor — it introduces the topic (remote work altering commuting patterns) without referring back to anything prior. The correct order is C → B → A → D: (B) follows with "as a result" directly building on the pattern shift introduced in (C); (A) follows referencing "this shift" (the occupancy decline just described in B); (D) follows logically as the planning implication elaborated from (A)'s "implications for urban planning."

**Q4 (Phrase replacement pattern — collocation).** "The manager decided to give a warning to the employee for repeated tardiness." Is "give a warning" the standard collocation, or is there a more standard replacement?
"Issue a warning" is generally the more standard, formal collocation in exam-standard English, though "give a warning" is not strictly ungrammatical — this illustrates that phrase replacement questions often test the MOST standard/precise collocation among options that may all be grammatically acceptable, rather than testing a clear grammar violation, which is why exposure to formal register phrasing specifically (not just grammar rules) matters for this question type.

**Q5 (Double-blank fill-in pattern — relationship check).** "Despite the ___1___ criticism, the CEO remained ___2___ about the company's long-term strategy." Which pair correctly completes this sentence: (a) "mild / devastated" (b) "scathing / steadfast" (c) "scathing / devastated"?
(b) "scathing / steadfast" is correct — it forms a coherent "despite" relationship: strong, harsh criticism ("scathing") met with firm resolve ("steadfast"), which is exactly what "despite" signals (a contrast between the criticism's intensity and the CEO's unwavering position). Option (a) fails because mild criticism wouldn't typically produce devastation, breaking the internal logic. Option (c) fails because "scathing" criticism producing "devastated" doesn't fit the "despite... remained" structure, which implies resilience against the criticism, not being overcome by it — testing whether the candidate checks the RELATIONSHIP between the two blanks, not just whether each fits its immediate local grammar.

**Q6 (RC pattern — inference vs. detail distinction).** A passage states: "Economists remain divided on whether the policy will achieve its stated employment targets, though most agree it will have some positive short-term effect." A question asks: "According to the passage, what is the economists' consensus view on the policy's long-term employment impact?" What's the correct approach to this question?
This is a detail/factual question, but requires careful reading rather than a shortcut — the passage explicitly states economists are DIVIDED on the long-term employment target outcome, while there IS agreement on short-term positive effect. A candidate who misreads "most agree it will have some positive short-term effect" as consensus on the LONG-TERM employment target specifically (conflating the two distinct claims in the sentence) would answer incorrectly — this illustrates why even "factual" questions require precise reading of exactly what the passage claims, distinguishing between what's stated with consensus versus what's stated as divided opinion.
