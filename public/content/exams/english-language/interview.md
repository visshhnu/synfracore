# English Language — Practice Q&A

**Q: Why does the "read questions before the passage" strategy work for some RC question types but not others?**
A: It saves time specifically on factual/detail questions, where the answer is a specific, locatable fact in the passage — skimming the questions first tells you what to actively look for while reading. It does NOT work for inference or tone-based questions, which require genuinely understanding the passage's overall argument and reasoning, not locating one specific sentence. A candidate who skims both the questions and the passage, expecting the shortcut to work universally, handles inference questions poorly because that shortcut specifically bypasses the full comprehension those questions require.

**Q: Explain why relying on a memorized dictionary definition can lead to wrong answers on vocabulary-in-context questions.**
A: Many words have multiple, sometimes near-opposite meanings depending on context or regional usage — "table" as a verb can mean either "postpone" or "formally present," depending on usage. Relying purely on whichever definition comes to mind first, without checking it against the actual sentence, risks selecting the wrong sense. The reliable method is re-substituting each answer option back into the original sentence and checking which one makes the sentence's overall meaning coherent in that specific context.

**Q: How would you systematically solve a para jumble question, rather than relying on intuition about what "sounds right"?**
A: Start by identifying the anchor sentence — the one that introduces the topic without depending on prior context, typically recognizable by the absence of backward-referencing language like "however," "this," or "such." Then work through the remaining sentences by looking for linking words that reveal which sentence each one logically follows. This turns the task into a systematic, checkable process rather than a vague sense of what flows well, and it also explains why a variant with the first and last sentences already given is actually easier, not harder, than a full jumble — two anchor points are already fixed.

**Q: What's the recurring trap pattern in subject-verb agreement error-detection questions, and how do you avoid falling for it?**
A: Exam sentences deliberately place a plural noun physically close to the verb — inside a prepositional phrase, or right after "each/every" — specifically to tempt a candidate into agreeing the verb with that nearby plural noun rather than the sentence's true grammatical subject. "The list of items IS on the table" is correct because "list" (singular) is the actual subject, not "items." The fix is deliberately identifying the true subject of the sentence before checking verb agreement, rather than agreeing with whatever noun happens to sit closest to the verb.

**Q: What's the difference between error detection and sentence improvement, and why is sentence improvement considered a harder skill?**
A: Error detection asks which part of a sentence is grammatically wrong — there's typically one clearly incorrect element among otherwise correct distractors. Sentence improvement, more common at the Mains level, presents several options that may ALL be grammatically valid, and the task is identifying the most precise or idiomatically standard phrasing among them. This requires genuine familiarity with natural, correctly-varied English phrasing — built through wide exposure to well-edited formal prose — rather than rule-based grammar knowledge alone, since the distinguishing factor is often idiom and precision, not a rule violation.

**Q: Why is logging vocabulary words with their original context sentence more valuable than logging just the word and its dictionary definition?**
A: Many exam-relevant words are context-dependent in meaning, and an isolated definition loses exactly the information needed to correctly apply that word's meaning when it appears in a different sentence later. Logging the actual sentence the word appeared in preserves that contextual information, building genuine contextual fluency rather than an isolated word-definition list that may not transfer correctly to a new sentence using the same word in a different sense.

**Q: A candidate encounters a genuinely dense, unfamiliar-topic RC passage during a Mains-level mock and is taking much longer than their planned time budget. What should they do?**
A: Recognize within the first couple of minutes that the passage is proving significantly harder than typical, and make a deliberate strategic choice rather than forcing completion within the original time budget at the cost of rushed, error-prone answers elsewhere. This often means ensuring strong performance on the remaining, more tractable sections first, and returning to the difficult passage with whatever time remains — the same selective-attempting principle that applies to disproportionately hard puzzles in the reasoning section, applied here to a genuinely difficult passage.
