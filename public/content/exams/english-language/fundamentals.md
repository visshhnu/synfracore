# English Language — Fundamentals

## Reading Comprehension: applying the question-first strategy correctly

The Overview tab's guidance — read questions before the passage for factual/detail questions — needs a specific execution method to actually save time rather than just shuffling where time is spent:

```
Step 1: Skim the questions first (not the options, just the
  question stems) — this tells you what to actively look FOR
  while reading, e.g. "what does the author say about X policy"

Step 2: Read the full passage once, actively noting (mentally
  or with a quick margin mark) where content relevant to each
  skimmed question appears

Step 3: For factual/detail questions, go directly to the noted
  location rather than re-reading the full passage
Step 3b: For inference/tone questions, these require genuine
  understanding of the passage's overall argument — a targeted
  re-read of one paragraph won't substitute for having actually
  understood the passage's full logic during step 2
```
The key distinction worth internalizing early: this strategy saves time on factual/detail questions specifically (where the answer is a specific, locatable fact) but does NOT eliminate the need to genuinely read and understand the passage for inference or tone-based questions, which require synthesizing the passage's overall argument, not locating one sentence. A candidate who skims questions first and then also skims the passage, expecting the shortcut to work for every question type, will handle inference questions poorly — the Overview tab's step 2 (read the full passage once) is not optional, it's specifically what makes inference questions answerable at all.

## Vocabulary-in-context: why re-substitution beats dictionary memorization

```
Sentence: "The committee's decision to table the proposal
  frustrated many members."

Dictionary meaning of "table" (as a verb, US usage): to
  POSTPONE discussion of something
Dictionary meaning of "table" (as a verb, UK/Indian usage,
  common in exam context): to formally PRESENT something for
  discussion — nearly the OPPOSITE sense

Correct approach: re-substitute each answer option back into
  the original sentence and check which one makes the sentence's
  overall meaning coherent in context — "frustrated many
  members" fits the POSTPONE sense far better than the PRESENT
  sense, which would typically be a neutral or positive action
```
This "table" example is a genuinely real illustration of why relying purely on a memorized dictionary definition, without re-substituting into context, causes real errors — many common exam vocabulary words have multiple, sometimes near-opposite meanings depending on regional usage or context, and only checking which meaning makes the *surrounding sentence* coherent (not just which definition you happen to remember first) reliably produces the correct answer.

## Para jumbles: finding the anchor sentence first

```
Scrambled sentences: 
  (A) This trend, however, is not without its critics.
  (B) Remote work adoption has accelerated significantly
      since 2020.
  (C) Critics argue that in-person collaboration remains
      irreplaceable for certain types of creative work.
  (D) Companies across sectors have restructured their
      operations accordingly.

Finding the anchor: (B) is the ANCHOR — it introduces the
  topic (remote work adoption) without referring back to
  anything, which makes it the natural opening sentence

Building forward: (D) logically follows (B) — "accordingly"
  refers back to the trend just introduced
  (A) follows (D) — "this trend, however" signals a pivot,
  referring back to the trend already established
  (C) follows (A) — "critics argue" elaborates on "critics"
  just mentioned in (A)

Order: B → D → A → C
```
The systematic method worth building: identify the anchor sentence first (the one that introduces a topic without depending on prior context — often signaled by NOT containing a pronoun, "however," "this," or similar backward-referencing language), then look for linking words in remaining sentences ("however," "this," "accordingly," "such") that reveal which sentence each one logically follows — this transforms para jumbles from guesswork into a systematic, checkable process, rather than relying purely on a vague sense of what "sounds right."

## Cloze test: reading the full passage before filling any blank

```
Common mistake: filling each blank as you encounter it,
  reading only the immediate sentence around that blank

Better approach: read through the FULL passage first (with
  blanks skipped), to understand the passage's overall tone
  and argument, THEN return to fill each blank with that full
  context in mind — a word that fits grammatically in an
  isolated sentence can still be wrong if it doesn't match
  the passage's overall tone (e.g., filling a blank with a
  neutral word when the passage's established tone is clearly
  critical)
```
This mirrors the Reading Comprehension principle directly — understanding the full passage before answering detail-level questions (here, individual blanks) prevents a technically grammatical but contextually wrong answer, which is a specific, common trap in cloze tests where multiple options might be grammatically valid in isolation but only one genuinely fits the passage's established tone and argument.

## Building a personalized grammar error log, not generic revision

The Overview tab's guidance to maintain a personalized list of grammar rules you personally get wrong, rather than generic revision, reflects a real efficiency principle: generic grammar revision spends equal time on rules you already handle correctly and rules that are genuinely your specific weak points, while a personalized error log concentrates all revision time on your actual, confirmed gaps. Building this log requires the same discipline as the mock-analysis habit covered in this platform's other exam-prep content — recording not just "got this wrong" but the specific rule category (subject-verb agreement with collective nouns, a specific preposition pairing) each time an error-spotting question is missed, and reviewing that log's recurring patterns specifically, is what makes grammar revision time genuinely efficient rather than diffusely spread across material regardless of actual need.
