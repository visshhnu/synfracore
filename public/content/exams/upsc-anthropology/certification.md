# UPSC Anthropology Optional — Scoring & What Separates Good from Average

There is no external "certification" for a UPSC optional subject — you don't get a credential for studying Anthropology the way you would for a technical certification exam. What matters instead is exactly how UPSC scores this optional and what measurably different behaviour separates candidates who score well (typically 300+/500 combined) from those who score average-to-poor (typically low-to-mid 200s/500), even when both groups know a similar volume of content.

## How the optional is actually scored

- Two papers, 250 marks each, 500 marks total — folded directly into the overall Mains merit ranking alongside the four GS papers and the Essay paper.
- Each paper: 8 questions across two sections, 5 to be attempted, Question 1 and Question 5 compulsory, at least one attempt from each section `(needs verification — recheck the exact current-year question format/compulsory-question rule against the latest official notification, since this detail can shift year to year independent of the syllabus)`.
- There is no negative marking on Mains descriptive papers — an incomplete or partially correct answer still earns partial credit, so leaving a question blank is close to the worst option, worse than attempting it imperfectly.
- Examiners mark holistically against a model answer/scheme, but with real weight given to naming the correct thinker, theory, biological term, or Indian concept/community, conceptual accuracy, and evidence of applying theory to a real example (not just defining a term) — this qualitative emphasis (not a hard numeric formula) is well-documented across aspirant/coaching post-result analyses `(needs verification — the general shape of this is well established but exact marking-scheme weightage is not publicly quantified by UPSC and should be treated as informed pattern, not official rule)`.
- Diagrams (kinship charts, evolutionary trees, genetic crosses, chromosome diagrams) are explicitly expected wherever relevant and are commonly reported by successful candidates and coaching sources as carrying real marks weight when drawn cleanly and correctly labelled — this is a distinguishing feature of Anthropology's scoring compared to purely discursive optionals like History or Public Administration `(needs verification — diagram-specific marks weightage is not separately published by UPSC and is an aspirant/coaching-reported pattern, not an official rule)`.

## What separates a good scorer from an average one

### 1. Naming a thinker, biological term, or Indian concept/community in nearly every paragraph
Average scorers explain a concept in plain, textbook-summary language — essentially a longer GS1 answer. Good scorers reach for a specific named thinker (Tylor, Malinowski, Radcliffe-Brown), a specific biological/genetic term (Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, polygenic inheritance), or a specific Indian concept/community (the tribe-caste continuum, Sanskritization, a named PVTG) *by default* whenever a topic has any theoretical, biological, or ethnographic dimension. This is the single most consistently cited differentiator among strong Anthropology scorers, because it is what actually distinguishes an "optional subject" answer from a general-knowledge governance or society answer on the same topic.

### 2. Precise, correctly labelled diagrams as part of the answer, not decoration
A kinship answer that only describes descent rules in prose scores lower than the same answer with a correctly drawn and labelled kinship chart integrated into the response. Good scorers treat diagram practice (Practice Set 4 in the Projects section) as core exam preparation, not an optional extra, and can produce a clean version of the standard diagrams (kinship charts, hominid evolutionary trees, basic genetic crosses) within the time a 15-20 mark question allows.

### 3. Applying theory to a real, current Indian example, not just defining it
"The tribe-caste continuum describes an intermediate zone between tribe and caste" scores lower than showing how that exact concept plays out in a real, named Indian region or community, or how a named theory (structural-functionalism) explains a specific Indian institution's persistence. Average answers stop at definition; good answers use the applied example as the analytical payload, then reach a defensible conclusion about the concept's actual relevance rather than leaving it purely abstract.

### 4. Time discipline and balanced coverage across both papers
Because there's no negative marking, a common average-scorer mistake is over-investing in Paper II's more "interesting" tribal-welfare content while under-preparing Paper I's genetics and evolution units, or vice versa for science-background candidates who neglect Paper II's social-cultural depth. Good scorers allocate time strictly by marks (roughly 1 minute per mark) and maintain working command of both papers so they can select the 5 strongest questions from the full 8 offered on exam day, rather than being constrained to whichever topics happen to fall in their comfort zone.

### 5. Currency on tribal-administration and welfare-policy content
Because Paper II's tribal-administration unit (Unit 9) and several vulnerable-sections topics explicitly reward current, real examples (a recent PVTG welfare initiative, a recent Forest Rights Act implementation development, a recent Scheduled Tribe status decision), candidates who treat the syllabus as entirely static and skip current-affairs integration tend to underperform on these specific questions relative to candidates who maintain a running current-affairs file mapped to syllabus units.
