# UPSC Anthropology Optional — Practice Sets

These are practice **prompts**, not model answers — the point is to build your own answer-writing reflexes (naming a thinker, a biological term, or an Indian concept/community, drawing a clean diagram, and taking a defensible position) under realistic conditions. Full model answers with examiner-level marking are handled by a separate premium feature; this section gives you the structured exercises to practice against.

## Practice Set 1: Thinker-and-Theory Drills (Paper I)

**Level:** Beginner | **Time:** 10-15 minutes per drill

Anthropology rewards naming a specific thinker, theory, or precise biological term, not a vague "anthropologists believe" framing. This drill builds that reflex directly.

### Drill instructions
1. Pick one topic from the list below.
2. In writing, name at least one specific thinker or named theory relevant to it, and one specific critique or limitation of that view.
3. Check yourself: did you name a real, specific thinker/term and a real critique, or did you just write "scholars believe"?

### Topics to rotate through weekly
- Tylor's definition of culture and the unilinear evolutionism it belonged to.
- Malinowski's functionalism versus Radcliffe-Brown's structural-functionalism.
- Morgan's classification of kinship terminology.
- The Hardy-Weinberg law and what causes deviation from it.
- The concept of "race" as a biological classification and why it is now considered scientifically weak.

### Skill demonstrated
Fluency in thinker-and-term citation, the single most consistently rewarded habit across every Paper I unit from Meaning and Scope through Applications of Anthropology.

## Practice Set 2: Answer-Writing Practice — Paper I (Theory and Biology)

**Level:** Intermediate | **Time:** 20-25 minutes per question, simulate real exam timing

Work through these UPSC-style prompts one at a time. Write a full structured answer (introduction defining the scope → body with 2-4 dimensions, naming at least one thinker/theory/term → conclusion) within the time limit implied by the marks. Where relevant, practice drawing the diagram (a kinship chart, a hominid family tree, a basic genetic cross) under the same time pressure.

### Sample prompts (practice these — do not just read them)
1. *(15 marks / ~20 min)* "Discuss E.B. Tylor's definition of culture. How has the concept of culture evolved since?" (Unit 2.1: The Nature of Culture)
2. *(15 marks / ~20 min)* "Distinguish between Malinowski's functionalism and Radcliffe-Brown's structural-functionalism, with examples." (Unit 6: Anthropological Theories)
3. *(20 marks / ~25 min)* "Describe the principles of descent and draw and label the basic types of descent groups." (Unit 2.5: Kinship)
4. *(15 marks / ~20 min)* "Trace the phylogenetic status of the major hominid fossils from Australopithecines to Homo sapiens." (Unit 1: Human Evolution)
5. *(20 marks / ~25 min)* "Explain the Hardy-Weinberg law. What factors cause deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium in a real population?" (Unit 9.3: Genetic Polymorphism and Selection)
6. *(15 marks / ~20 min)* "Critically examine the concept of race as a biological category. Is racial classification scientifically valid today?" (Unit 9.5: Race and Racism)
7. *(15 marks / ~20 min)* "Discuss the significance of participant observation in the fieldwork tradition of Anthropology." (Unit 8: Research Methods)

### After each attempt, self-check against
- Did the introduction define the key term or scope in one or two lines, without padding?
- Did you name at least one specific thinker, theory, or precise biological/genetic term, not just describe a concept in plain prose?
- Did the body cover at least 2-3 distinct dimensions or perspectives, not just one long descriptive list?
- If a diagram was relevant, did you draw and label it clearly, not just describe it in words?
- Does the conclusion add a judgment (a synthesis, a limitation, an applied example) rather than just repeating the introduction?

## Practice Set 3: Answer-Writing Practice — Paper II (Indian Anthropology)

**Level:** Intermediate/Advanced | **Time:** 20-25 minutes per question

### Sample prompts
1. *(20 marks)* "Discuss the tribe-caste continuum in India with suitable examples." (Unit 3.2: Caste System in India)
2. *(15 marks)* "Examine the concept of Sanskritization. How adequate is it in explaining social change in India today?" (Unit 5.3: Indigenous and Exogenous Processes of Socio-Cultural Change)
3. *(20 marks)* "Discuss the concept of Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs) and the challenges in their development." (Unit 9: Tribal Administration and Development)
4. *(15 marks)* "Examine the impact of large development projects on tribal communities in India." (Unit 6.3: Developmental Projects and Their Impact)
5. *(15 marks)* "Discuss the theories of origin of the caste system in India." (Unit 3.2: Caste System in India)
6. *(20 marks)* "Critically examine the constitutional safeguards available to Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in India." (Unit 7.1: Problems of Exploitation and Deprivation)
7. *(15 marks)* "Discuss the concept of the sacred complex with reference to L.P. Vidyarthi's work." (Unit 3.3: Sacred Complex and Nature-Man-Spirit Complex)

### After each attempt, self-check against
- Did you name at least one specific Indian anthropological concept, named community, or constitutional/policy provision rather than describing the topic generically?
- Did you connect the answer back to a Paper I theoretical concept (structural-functionalism, cultural relativism, evolution) where relevant?
- Did the conclusion offer a reasoned judgment or a note on the concept's continuing relevance/limitation, not just a summary of facts?

## Practice Set 4: Kinship Diagram and Concept-Index Building (Capstone)

**Level:** Advanced | **Time:** Ongoing, one major topic per week

This is the highest-leverage practice exercise for both papers, because a single well-built diagram or index table can supply citable, drawable material for multiple questions on the same topic.

### Steps
1. Choose one major cross-cutting theme per week (kinship systems, human evolution, caste and tribe, tribal administration — rotate to cover both papers over your prep).
2. For Paper I kinship/biology themes, practice redrawing the relevant diagram from memory (a kinship chart with correct symbols, a hominid evolutionary tree, a basic Mendelian cross) until you can produce a clean, labelled version in under five minutes.
3. For Paper II themes, build a table: concept/community name, its precise definition, one real Indian example, and one live policy or administrative detail connected to it.
4. Cross-reference each entry back to the specific unit and sub-topic it belongs to, so the material stays retrievable under exam conditions.
5. After building 6-8 topic tables/diagram sets, attempt a comparative question (e.g., "Compare functionalism and structural-functionalism," or "Compare the tribal situation in two different regions of India") using only your own notes and diagrams — no external lookups — to test how usable your preparation actually is under exam conditions.

### Skill demonstrated
Synthesis of theoretical and ethnographic material, comparative analysis, precise diagram execution, and exam-ready recall organised by citable evidence rather than generic description.
