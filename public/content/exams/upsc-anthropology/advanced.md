# UPSC Anthropology Optional — Advanced (Paper II: Indian Anthropology)

Paper II is a set of 9 numbered units covering India's actual ethnographic, social, and tribal-administrative reality. This is the exact structure and topic list as given in UPSC's official syllabus.

## The full Paper II syllabus

```
1. (1.1-1.3) EVOLUTION OF INDIAN CULTURE AND CIVILIZATION:
   Prehistoric, Protohistoric (Indus Civilization), Pre-Harappan, Harappan
     and post-Harappan cultures; contributions of tribal cultures to
     Indian civilization
   Palaeo-anthropological evidence from India with special reference to
     Siwaliks and Narmada basin
   Ethno-archaeology in India: the concept; survivals and parallels among
     hunting, foraging, fishing, pastoral and peasant communities

2. DEMOGRAPHIC PROFILE OF INDIA:
   Ethnic and linguistic elements and their distribution
   Factors influencing population structure and growth

3. (3.1-3.4) THE INDIAN SOCIAL SYSTEM:
   The structure and nature of the traditional Indian social system:
     Varnashrama, Purushartha, Karma, Rina and Rebirth
   Caste system in India: structure and characteristics; theories of
     origin; dominant caste; caste mobility; Jajmani system; tribe-caste
     continuum
   Sacred Complex and Nature-Man-Spirit Complex
   Impact of Buddhism, Jainism, Islam and Christianity on Indian society

4. EMERGENCE, GROWTH AND DEVELOPMENT OF ANTHROPOLOGY IN INDIA:
   Contributions of scholars and Indian anthropologists to tribal and
     caste studies

5. (5.1-5.3) INDIAN VILLAGE AND SOCIAL CHANGE:
   Indian Village: significance; as a social system; traditional and
     changing patterns; agrarian relations; impact of globalization
   Linguistic and religious minorities and their social, political and
     economic status
   Indigenous and exogenous processes of socio-cultural change:
     Sanskritization, Westernization, Modernization; Panchayati Raj; role
     of media and social change

6. (6.1-6.3) TRIBAL SITUATION IN INDIA:
   Bio-genetic variability, linguistic and socio-economic characteristics
     and distribution of tribal communities
   Problems of the tribal communities: land alienation, poverty,
     indebtedness, low literacy, health and nutrition
   Developmental projects and their impact on tribal displacement and
     rehabilitation; forest policy and tribals; impact of urbanisation
     and industrialisation on tribal communities

7. (7.1-7.3) VULNERABLE SECTIONS AND ETHNICITY:
   Problems of exploitation and deprivation of Scheduled Castes,
     Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes; constitutional
     safeguards
   Social change among tribal communities: impact of democratic
     institutions, development programmes and welfare measures
   The concept of ethnicity; ethnic conflicts; regionalism;
     pseudo-tribalism; social change during colonial and post-Independent
     India

8. (8.1-8.2) TRIBE, RELIGION AND NATION:
   Impact of Hinduism, Buddhism, Christianity, Islam and other religions
     on tribal societies
   Tribe and nation state: a comparative study of tribal communities in
     India and other countries

9. (9.1-9.3) TRIBAL ADMINISTRATION AND DEVELOPMENT:
   History of administration of tribal areas; tribal policies, plans,
     programmes; the concept of Primitive Tribal Groups (PTGs)/PVTGs and
     their programmes; role of NGOs
   Role of anthropology in tribal and rural development
   Contributions of anthropology to the understanding of regionalism,
     communalism, and ethnic and political movements
```

## How the nine units connect

Unit 1 supplies the deep-time foundation — how Indian civilization itself evolved from prehistoric and Harappan roots, with tribal cultures explicitly recognised as contributors to that civilizational story rather than a separate, parallel track. Unit 2 (Demographic Profile) gives the numeric and ethnic-linguistic map of the country the rest of the paper is set against. Unit 3 (The Indian Social System) is the paper's theoretical core on caste — Varnashrama, the caste system's structure and theories of origin, and the tribe-caste continuum that directly bridges tribal and caste studies. Unit 4 is a short but exam-relevant unit on the discipline's own Indian history — which scholars and administrator-anthropologists actually built the field in India. Unit 5 (Indian Village and Social Change) moves to the unit of analysis every Indian sociology and anthropology course treats as central — the village — and the mechanisms (Sanskritization, Westernization, Modernization) by which its social structure changes. Units 6-7 (Tribal Situation; Vulnerable Sections and Ethnicity) form the paper's highest-yield administrative core — the actual problems tribal communities face and the constitutional and policy response to them, alongside the wider category of Scheduled Castes and OBCs. Unit 8 (Tribe, Religion and Nation) widens the lens to religious impact on tribal societies and a comparative, cross-country view of tribal-state relations. Unit 9 (Tribal Administration and Development) closes the paper with the actual machinery — historical and current tribal-area administration, the PVTG concept, and anthropology's own applied role in development.

**High-yield linkage to memorise:** Units 6, 7, and 9 are functionally one continuous theme (tribal situation → the constitutional/welfare response → the administrative machinery) even though they are numbered as three separate units — a strong candidate builds one running note-set spanning all three, since a single question on "PVTGs" or "tribal displacement" routinely draws on content technically filed under all three unit numbers.

### How to actually study Unit 3.2 (Caste system) and the tribe-caste continuum

This is one of the two or three most consistently tested sub-topics in the entire optional, because it sits exactly at the joint between "pure" caste studies and "pure" tribal studies. Know the standard theories of caste origin (the traditional/religious theory rooted in the Varna model, the racial theory associating caste with distinct racial groups, the occupational theory linking caste to hereditary division of labour, and the political/Brahminical-dominance theories emphasising caste as a tool of social control) well enough to name and briefly critique each, rather than presenting caste origin as settled fact. Then know the **tribe-caste continuum** concept precisely: in large parts of peninsular and central India, many communities do not sit cleanly on either side of a tribe/caste binary but occupy an intermediate zone, gradually adopting caste-like practices (a process closely related to, but distinct from, Sanskritization) while retaining tribal features — a live, still-debated anthropological description of Indian social reality, not a settled historical event, so answers should present it as an analytical lens rather than a fixed classification.

## Cross-cutting exam skill: naming a precise Indian concept, community, or policy detail in every answer

Paper II's equivalent of Paper I's "name a thinker or biological term" discipline is naming a specific anthropological concept coined or standardised for the Indian context (Sanskritization, the tribe-caste continuum, the jajmani system, PVTGs), a specific named community or region as an illustrative example (the Santhal, the Naga tribes, the Bhil, a Fifth Schedule or Sixth Schedule area) where relevant, or a specific constitutional/policy detail (Article 342 on Scheduled Tribes, Fifth and Sixth Schedule administration, the Forest Rights Act) rather than describing a tribal or caste situation in generic prose. This is the direct Paper II analogue of Paper I's "name a theorist" discipline (Intermediate section) — a strong Paper II answer routinely does both, borrowing a Paper I theoretical frame (structural-functionalism, cultural relativism) and grounding it in a named Indian community or policy detail in the same paragraph.

## Study resources
- **Nadeem Hasnain, "Indian Society and Culture: Continuity and Change"** — a standard first read for Units 2-5 (demography, the Indian social system, village and social change).
- **Vidyarthi and Rai, "The Tribal Culture of India"** — comprehensive coverage of Units 6-9 (tribal situation, ethnicity, tribal administration).
- **L.P. Vidyarthi's "sacred complex" framework (originally developed in his study of Gaya)** — directly useful for Unit 3.3 (Sacred Complex and Nature-Man-Spirit Complex).
- **Ministry of Tribal Affairs annual reports and 2nd Administrative Reforms Commission-adjacent tribal-welfare material** — primary-source reading for Unit 9's tribal administration and PVTG content `(needs verification — treat scheme names and current PVTG counts as live administrative detail requiring a fresh check against the Ministry's current published material, not fixed syllabus content)`.
- **Previous Year Question papers (upsc.gov.in)** — the single most reliable guide to which sub-topics are tested most often (the tribe-caste continuum, PVTGs, and Sanskritization/Westernization are consistently high-yield).
