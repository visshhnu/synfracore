# UPSC Anthropology Optional — Intermediate (Paper I: Anthropology — Theory and Biology)

Paper I is a set of 12 numbered units covering universal anthropological theory and human biology — not country-specific, and not chronologically sequential the way History's units are. This is the exact structure and topic list as given in UPSC's official syllabus.

## The full Paper I syllabus

```
1. (1.1) MEANING, SCOPE AND DEVELOPMENT OF ANTHROPOLOGY:
   Relationships with other disciplines: Social Sciences, Behavioural
     Sciences, Life Sciences, Medical Sciences, Earth Sciences, Humanities
   Main branches of Anthropology, their scope and relevance:
     Social-cultural Anthropology, Biological Anthropology,
     Archaeological Anthropology, Linguistic Anthropology
   Human Evolution and emergence of Man:
     Biological and Cultural factors in human evolution
     Theories of Organic Evolution (Pre-Darwinian, Darwinian, Post-Darwinian)
     Synthetic theory of evolution; brief outline of terms and concepts
   Characteristics of Primates: evolutionary trend and primate taxonomy,
     primate adaptations, primate behaviour, fossil primates, living major
     primates, comparative anatomy of man and apes
   Phylogenetic status, characteristics and geographical distribution of
     hominids including Australopithecines, Homo erectus, Neanderthal man,
     Rhodesian man, Homo sapiens
   The biological basis of Life: the Cell, DNA structure and replication,
     Protein Synthesis, Gene, Mutation, Chromosomes, Cell Division
   Principles of Prehistoric Archaeology; Chronology: relative and
     absolute dating methods
   Cultural Evolution — broad outlines of Prehistoric cultures:
     Paleolithic, Mesolithic, Neolithic, Chalcolithic, Copper-Bronze Age,
     Iron Age

2. (2.1-2.5) SOCIAL INSTITUTIONS:
   The Nature of Culture: concept and characteristics of culture and
     civilization; ethnocentrism vis-a-vis cultural relativism
   The Nature of Society: concept of society; society and culture; social
     institutions; social groups; social stratification
   Marriage: definition and universality; laws of marriage; types of
     marriage; functions of marriage; marriage regulations; marriage
     payments
   Family: definition and universality; family, household and domestic
     groups; functions of family; types of family; impact of
     urbanization, industrialization and feminist movements
   Kinship: consanguinity and affinity; principles and types of descent;
     forms of descent groups; kinship terminology; descent and alliance

3. ECONOMIC ORGANIZATION:
   Meaning, scope and relevance
   Formalist and Substantivist debate
   Principles governing production, distribution and exchange in various
     communities

4. POLITICAL ORGANIZATION AND SOCIAL CONTROL:
   Band, tribe, chiefdom, kingdom and state
   Concepts of power, authority and legitimacy
   Social control, law and justice

5. RELIGION:
   Anthropological approaches to religion
   Monotheism and polytheism
   Sacred and profane
   Myths and rituals
   Forms of religion in tribal and peasant societies
   Magico-religious functionaries

6. ANTHROPOLOGICAL THEORIES:
   Classical evolutionism, Historical particularism, Diffusionism,
     Functionalism, Structural-Functionalism, Structuralism, Culture and
     personality, Neo-evolutionism, Cultural materialism, Symbolic and
     interpretive theories, Cognitive theories, Postmodernism

7. CULTURE, LANGUAGE AND COMMUNICATION:
   Nature, origin and characteristics of language
   Verbal and non-verbal communication
   Social context of language use

8. RESEARCH METHODS IN ANTHROPOLOGY:
   Fieldwork tradition
   Distinction between technique, method and methodology
   Tools of data collection
   Analysis, interpretation and presentation of data

9. (9.1-9.8) HUMAN GENETICS AND APPLIED ANTHROPOLOGY:
   Human Genetics: methods and application including family study,
     pedigree analysis, twin study, biochemical methods, DNA technology
   Mendelian genetics in man including single factor, multifactor, and
     polygenic inheritance
   Concept of genetic polymorphism and selection; Mendelian population;
     Hardy-Weinberg law; causes of frequency changes
   Chromosomes and chromosomal aberrations in man including numerical,
     structural, and sex chromosomal aberrations
   Race and racism; biological basis of morphological variation; racial
     criteria and classification
   Age, sex and population variation as genetic markers: ABO, Rh blood
     groups, HLA, physiological characteristics
   Concepts and methods of Ecological Anthropology: bio-cultural
     adaptations; man's physiological responses to environmental stresses
   Epidemiological Anthropology: health and disease; infectious and
     non-infectious diseases; nutritional deficiency-related diseases

10. HUMAN GROWTH AND DEVELOPMENT:
   Concept of human growth and development
   Stages of growth
   Factors affecting growth
   Ageing and senescence
   Human physique and somatotypes

11. (11.1-11.3) FERTILITY AND DEMOGRAPHY:
   Relevance of menarche, menopause and other bio-events to fertility;
     fertility patterns and differentials
   Demographic theories — biological, social and cultural
   Biological and socio-ecological factors influencing fecundity,
     fertility, natality and mortality

12. APPLICATIONS OF ANTHROPOLOGY:
   Anthropology of sports
   Nutritional anthropology
   Forensic Anthropology
   Applied human genetics
   DNA technology in diseases and medicine
```

## How the twelve units connect

Unit 1 is the discipline's own foundation — what Anthropology is, how its four branches relate, and, uniquely for Unit 1, a large embedded block of biological-anthropology content (human evolution, primates, hominid phylogeny, cell biology, and prehistoric archaeology) that essentially front-loads Paper I's science content before the syllabus turns to social institutions. Unit 2 covers the core social institutions (culture, society, marriage, family, kinship) that every social-cultural anthropology answer draws on. Units 3-5 (Economic Organization, Political Organization and Social Control, Religion) are the classic "institutional" units describing how non-state and tribal societies organise production, power, and belief. Unit 6 (Anthropological Theories) is the discipline's intellectual history in condensed form — the Fundamentals section's Tylor/Morgan/Malinowski/Radcliffe-Brown foundation is the entry point into this unit's full list of schools. Units 7-8 (Culture, Language and Communication; Research Methods) cover the discipline's own tools — how anthropologists study language and how fieldwork is actually conducted. Unit 9 returns to biology in depth — human genetics, population variation, race, ecological and epidemiological anthropology. Units 10-11 (Human Growth and Development; Fertility and Demography) are the biological-anthropology content most directly applicable to real population health and policy questions. Unit 12 (Applications of Anthropology) closes the paper with the discipline's real-world uses — sports, nutrition, forensics, medicine.

**High-yield linkage to memorise:** Unit 6's list of anthropological theories (Classical evolutionism through Postmodernism) is not a standalone memorisation task — nearly every theory named there was introduced, in practice, by a specific thinker covered conceptually in the Fundamentals section or commonly paired with one (Tylor/Morgan → Classical evolutionism; Boas → Historical particularism; Malinowski → Functionalism; Radcliffe-Brown → Structural-Functionalism; Lévi-Strauss → Structuralism; Julian Steward/Leslie White → Neo-evolutionism; Marvin Harris → Cultural materialism; Clifford Geertz → Symbolic/interpretive theories). Building one running "theory-thinker" index card — school name, associated thinker(s), one-line core claim — pays off directly on Unit 6 and indirectly across Units 2-5 wherever an institutional answer benefits from naming the theoretical lens being used.

### How to actually study Unit 9 (Human Genetics) if you have no science background

This is the unit non-science candidates most often under-prepare, because it looks like a full genetics course compressed into one syllabus line. The exam-tested core is narrower than it looks: know Mendelian inheritance patterns (single-factor, multifactor, polygenic) well enough to explain a basic cross; know what the Hardy-Weinberg law actually states (allele and genotype frequencies in a population remain constant across generations absent evolutionary forces, making it the baseline against which real population changes are measured) and what factors cause deviation from it (mutation, migration, genetic drift, selection); know the ABO and Rh blood group systems as the standard, most exam-tested example of population genetic markers; and know that modern anthropology treats "race" as a biologically weak, largely socially constructed classification, not a rigorous genetic category — this last point is one of the most consistently tested critical positions in the entire unit.

## Cross-cutting exam skill: naming a thinker, theory, or precise biological term in every answer

Anthropology has no compulsory map question and no single "named event" discipline like History's dates — but it has an equivalent discipline: every strong Paper I answer names at least one specific thinker (Tylor, Malinowski, Radcliffe-Brown, Lévi-Strauss), a specific theoretical school (structural-functionalism, cultural materialism), or a precise biological/genetic term (Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, polygenic inheritance, chromosomal aberration) rather than describing a concept in generic prose. Build this habit in Paper I first — Paper II rewards the identical discipline applied to named Indian communities, concepts, and welfare schemes instead of universal theorists and biological terms.

## Study resources
- **Nadeem Hasnain, "A Concise Introduction to Anthropology"** — standard first read covering Units 1-2 (Introduction, Social Institutions) and Unit 6 (Theories).
- **P. Nath / any standard undergraduate physical anthropology text** — for Units 1 (evolution/primates), 9 (genetics), 10-11 (growth/demography).
- **D.N. Majumdar and T.N. Madan, "An Introduction to Social Anthropology"** — strong for Units 2-5 (social institutions, economic/political organisation, religion).
- **Previous Year Question papers (upsc.gov.in)** — the single most reliable guide to which sub-topics within each unit are actually tested most often (kinship terminology, Hardy-Weinberg law, and the functionalism/structural-functionalism comparison are consistently high-yield).
