# GS Paper 4 — Fundamentals

## Applying ethics theories to an actual answer, not just naming them

Knowing the definitions of consequentialism, deontology, and virtue ethics (Overview tab) is necessary but insufficient — GS Paper 4 rewards actually applying them to reason through a specific dilemma, not reciting definitions:

```
A civil servant discovers a minor procedural violation by a
  senior colleague that, if reported, would delay an important
  welfare scheme reaching beneficiaries.

Consequentialist reasoning: weigh the outcome of reporting
  (delay in welfare delivery, harm to intended beneficiaries)
  against not reporting (procedural integrity compromised,
  potential precedent for larger future violations)

Deontological reasoning: the duty to report a violation exists
  independent of the consequences — following the rule/duty
  itself is the ethical action, regardless of the welfare
  scheme's timeline

Virtue ethics reasoning: what would a person of genuine
  integrity and practical wisdom (phronesis) actually do here —
  likely seeking a path that addresses the violation without
  necessarily causing the worst-case delay (escalating
  internally first, for instance, rather than an immediate
  public report)
```
A strong answer doesn't just apply one theory and stop — it demonstrates awareness that different ethical frameworks can point toward different conclusions on the same dilemma, and explicitly reasons through this tension before arriving at a justified position, rather than mechanically naming one theory and treating that as sufficient analysis. This is the actual skill Paper 4 tests: reasoned ethical judgment under genuine competing considerations, not theory recall.

## Integrity in public service: making the abstract values concrete

```
The Overview tab's five values (Probity, Objectivity,
  Accountability, Transparency, Non-partisanship) are easy to
  list but need concrete illustration in an answer to actually
  demonstrate understanding, not just naming:

Probity — concretely: refusing a gift from a contractor bidding
  on a project you're evaluating, even when the gift is modest
  and framed as a "gesture of goodwill"

Objectivity — concretely: recommending the technically superior
  bid in a tender process even when a personally preferred
  vendor submitted a weaker proposal

Accountability — concretely: proactively informing a superior
  about an error in an already-submitted report, rather than
  hoping it goes unnoticed

Transparency — concretely: publishing the criteria used for a
  discretionary allocation decision BEFORE the decision is made,
  not just justifying it after the fact if questioned
```
An answer that lists "probity, objectivity, accountability" without any concrete illustration reads as memorized vocabulary rather than genuine understanding — pairing each abstract value with a specific, realistic scenario (even a brief one) is what demonstrates the examiner is looking for: applied comprehension, not definitional recall.

## Case study answer structure: understanding WHY each step exists

The Overview tab's 5-step case study format isn't an arbitrary template — each step serves a specific analytical purpose worth understanding, not just following mechanically:

```
1. Identify ethical issues/conflicts — establishes you've
   correctly recognized WHAT is actually at stake, before
   proposing any solution (a common weak-answer pattern:
   jumping straight to a solution without first demonstrating
   you've identified the genuine ethical tension)

2. List stakeholders and impact — demonstrates comprehensive
   thinking beyond the immediate parties directly named in the
   case — a strong answer identifies stakeholders NOT explicitly
   mentioned in the prompt (e.g., future beneficiaries, public
   trust in the institution) that a weaker answer misses

3. Present 3-4 options with pros/cons — demonstrates genuine
   deliberation rather than assuming the "obvious" answer from
   the start; even options ultimately rejected should be
   presented with genuine consideration, not as strawmen

4. Choose with ethical justification — the actual decision,
   grounded explicitly in the theories/values from steps 1-3,
   not introduced as a fresh, unjustified assertion

5. Short and long-term consequences — demonstrates the
   judgment extends beyond immediate resolution to genuine
   downstream thinking, which is often where a strong answer
   distinguishes itself from an adequate one
```
Understanding each step's actual purpose (not just its label) is what prevents an answer from becoming a mechanical checklist run-through — an examiner can distinguish an answer that's genuinely reasoning through each step's purpose from one that's superficially hitting the format's headings without real depth underneath each one.

## Emotional intelligence: the four components, applied to public service specifically

```
Self-awareness — recognizing your own biases before they
  influence a decision (e.g., recognizing personal frustration
  with a specific community before making a discretionary
  decision affecting them)

Self-management — maintaining composure and sound judgment
  under the genuine pressure public service roles often involve
  (an angry public delegation, political pressure)

Social awareness — genuinely understanding a community's or
  colleague's actual perspective and constraints, not assuming
  your own frame of reference applies universally

Relationship management — building genuine trust and
  cooperation with stakeholders, especially those with whom
  there's inherent tension (e.g., a regulator and the industry
  it regulates)
```
Goleman's four-component EI framework (Overview tab) is worth connecting explicitly to public service scenarios in an answer, since the framework's abstract components map directly onto genuinely common civil service situations — a strong answer demonstrates this connection specifically, rather than treating EI as a separate, disconnected topic from the practical scenarios the exam actually asks about.
