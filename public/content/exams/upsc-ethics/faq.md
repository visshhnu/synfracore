# GS Paper 4 — FAQ

**Do I need to memorize exact quotes from Kant, Mill, and other thinkers to score well?**
No — what matters is genuinely understanding and being able to apply each thinker's core framework to a specific scenario, not reciting exact quotes. An answer that correctly applies Kantian duty-based reasoning to a case, in your own words, scores better than one that quotes Kant precisely but doesn't actually connect the quote to the specific dilemma being analyzed.

**Is there always one "correct" answer to a case study, or is the exam testing something else?**
Case studies are deliberately designed to present genuine tension between legitimate values, not a single obviously correct answer — the exam is testing your ability to reason through that tension and arrive at a well-justified position, not your ability to identify the "right" answer among clearly wrong alternatives. Two candidates can reach different final decisions on the same case study and both score well, provided each reasoning process is genuine, thorough, and properly justified.

**Should I always choose the "safest" or most conservative option in a case study to avoid controversial-seeming answers?**
Not necessarily — examiners reward genuine, well-reasoned judgment more than a consistently cautious default. An answer that mechanically picks the safest-seeming option every time, without genuinely weighing the specific facts of each case, can read as avoiding real ethical engagement rather than demonstrating sound judgment. The goal is a justified position grounded in the specific scenario's actual considerations, not a formulaic "always play it safe" pattern.

**How much should I worry about running out of time and having to skip parts of the case study method?**
Prioritize compressing your WRITING for each of the 5 steps over skipping steps entirely — each step is allocated marks for a distinct demonstrated skill, so an answer that briefly but genuinely addresses all 5 steps typically scores better than one that develops only 2-3 steps in greater depth. Practicing writing more concisely at each step, through timed practice, is a better time-management fix than abandoning the method's structure under pressure.

**Is it acceptable to acknowledge that an ethically correct decision might have negative personal consequences for the decision-maker?**
Yes, and doing so genuinely strengthens an answer rather than weakening it — acknowledging real institutional or personal costs (career risk, strained relationships) while still reasoning to the conclusion that an action was ethically warranted despite those costs demonstrates more mature, realistic ethical reasoning than pretending the correct choice is automatically easy or costless. Examiners generally recognize and reward this honesty over an answer that implausibly presents ethics as always conflict-free.

**Why does the exam ask about corruption types (grand vs. petty vs. state capture) instead of just testing whether corruption is wrong?**
Because the exam is testing applied administrative judgment, not a basic moral stance everyone already agrees with — the actually useful skill for a future civil servant is recognizing which TYPE of corruption a given situation represents and proposing a response calibrated to that type's actual scale and mechanism (individual integrity for petty corruption, institutional/systemic response for grand corruption or state capture). This distinction matters practically because a mismatched response — treating a systemic problem as fixable through individual honesty alone, for instance — genuinely fails to address the real scope of many corruption scenarios encountered in actual administrative practice.
