# GS Paper 4 — Intermediate

## A fully worked case study, following the 5-step method

```
Case: You are a District Collector. A powerful local politician
  requests you fast-track approval of a construction project
  that technically meets minimum requirements but raises genuine
  environmental concerns flagged by your technical team. Refusing
  risks political retaliation affecting your career; approving
  risks environmental harm and sets a precedent for future
  pressure.

STEP 1 — Ethical issues: conflict between political pressure
  and professional/environmental duty; potential precedent-
  setting for future discretionary pressure; personal career
  risk versus institutional integrity; public trust in
  environmental clearance processes.

STEP 2 — Stakeholders: the local community (environmental
  impact), the politician (political interests), your own
  career/institutional standing, future affected communities
  if precedent is set, the technical team whose assessment is
  at stake, broader public trust in environmental governance.

STEP 3 — Options:
  (a) Approve immediately — pro: avoids political conflict; con:
      compromises environmental duty, sets bad precedent
  (b) Refuse outright — pro: upholds environmental integrity;
      con: may not address genuinely fixable technical concerns,
      risks unnecessary conflict if concerns are actually minor
  (c) Request additional technical review with a defined
      timeline, communicated transparently to all parties — pro:
      grounds the decision in technical merit rather than either
      pure compliance or pure refusal; con: takes more time,
      may still face pressure during the review period

STEP 4 — Chosen action with justification: Option (c) —
  grounds the decision in objectivity and procedural
  transparency (Overview tab's integrity values) rather than
  either capitulating to pressure or making a unilateral refusal
  without full technical grounding; demonstrates accountability
  by documenting the reasoning transparently.

STEP 5 — Consequences: Short-term — political friction is
  likely regardless of outcome, but transparent process reduces
  perception of arbitrary decision-making. Long-term — establishes
  a precedent for evidence-based decision-making resistant to
  future political pressure, strengthening institutional
  credibility over time, even if a specific short-term relationship
  is strained.
```
This worked example illustrates the actual depth expected at each step — not just naming "stakeholders" but genuinely identifying the less-obvious ones (future affected communities, the technical team's standing), and not just picking an option but grounding the choice explicitly in the integrity values and ethical reasoning developed in earlier steps, rather than introducing the final decision as an unconnected assertion.

## Balancing genuinely conflicting values, not choosing an obvious "correct" one

```
A recurring theme in Mains-level case studies: values that are
  ALL genuinely legitimate can conflict directly — this is
  deliberate, testing judgment under genuine tension, not
  testing whether a candidate can identify a clearly "correct"
  answer

Common genuine tensions:
  Compassion vs. rule-following (a technically ineligible but
    genuinely deserving welfare applicant)
  Loyalty to a superior vs. institutional integrity (asked to
    approve something you believe is wrong)
  Short-term public good vs. long-term institutional precedent
    (a popular but procedurally irregular decision)
```
A weak answer resolves this tension by simply asserting one value trumps the other without genuine reasoning ("integrity always comes first") — a strong answer explicitly acknowledges BOTH values as legitimate, reasons through the specific weight each deserves in THIS particular scenario's specific facts, and arrives at a position that's justified by that reasoning rather than a generic, context-independent rule. Examiners specifically reward this demonstrated tension-navigation over a confident but under-reasoned assertion of one value's supremacy.

## Corruption: distinguishing types, since the appropriate response differs by type

```
Grand corruption — large-scale, systemic abuse of high-level
  power (e.g., major procurement fraud) — typically requires
  institutional/systemic response (whistleblowing mechanisms,
  audit systems), individual action alone is often insufficient

Petty corruption — small-scale, often routine (e.g., a bribe
  for a routine service) — individual integrity and refusal
  matters directly, alongside systemic fixes (reducing
  discretionary points of contact, digitization reducing
  human-touchpoint corruption opportunities)

State capture — a more insidious form where private interests
  systematically influence policy-making itself, not just
  individual transactions — requires structural/institutional
  safeguards (transparency in policy-making, conflict-of-
  interest disclosure) rather than individual-level integrity
  alone, since it operates at a level individual honesty can't
  fully counter
```
Recognizing which type of corruption a case study scenario actually describes matters because the appropriate response genuinely differs — an answer proposing an individual-integrity-focused response to what's actually a state-capture scenario, or a systemic-institutional response to what's actually simple petty corruption, misses the specific, correct scale of intervention the scenario calls for.

## Constitutional morality: applying Ambedkar's concept concretely

```
Constitutional morality (Ambedkar) — adherence to the
  Constitution's underlying values (equality, justice, liberty,
  fraternity) even when they conflict with popular sentiment or
  majoritarian preference

Applied example: a civil servant implementing a policy that's
  constitutionally sound (protecting a minority's rights) but
  faces significant local popular opposition — constitutional
  morality provides the grounding for maintaining the policy
  despite the popular pressure, distinct from simple rule-
  following (deontology) because it's specifically grounded in
  the Constitution's foundational values, not just "the rules
  say so"
```
Distinguishing constitutional morality from simple rule-based deontology is a genuinely useful nuance for a strong answer — constitutional morality specifically invokes the deeper values a rule is meant to serve (equality, justice), which matters especially in a case where a technically-followable but value-violating action might otherwise seem procedurally correct; grounding a position in constitutional morality rather than mere rule-following demonstrates the deeper conceptual understanding examiners look for.
