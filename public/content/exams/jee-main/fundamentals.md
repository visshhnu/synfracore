# JEE Main — Fundamentals

## Understanding percentile normalization before it confuses you on results day

JEE Main runs across multiple shifts and days, and different shifts inevitably have slightly different difficulty levels despite NTA's efforts to keep them comparable — percentile normalization exists specifically to make scores comparable across this variation:

```
Your percentile = percentage of candidates in YOUR SPECIFIC SHIFT
  who scored equal to or below you — NOT a comparison against
  every candidate across all shifts/days directly

Example: a raw score of 250/300 might be the 99.5th percentile
  in a harder shift, but only 98.9th percentile in an easier
  shift where more candidates scored similarly high
```
The practical implication worth understanding before results day, not after: your raw marks number is genuinely less important than your percentile, and two candidates with different raw scores in different shifts can end up with very similar percentiles, or vice versa — a candidate fixating on comparing their raw score against a friend who took a different shift is comparing numbers that aren't directly comparable, which is a common, confusing misunderstanding worth avoiding from the start of preparation, not discovering for the first time when results are released.

## Numerical-value questions: the section where negative marking doesn't apply

```
Each subject: 20 MCQ (negative marking: -1 for wrong) +
              10 numerical-value questions, attempt any 5
              (NO negative marking on numerical-value questions)

This asymmetry directly changes optimal attempt strategy:
  MCQ section — apply the same expected-value guessing logic
    covered in this platform's NEET Mock Tests content (attempt
    only with genuine partial elimination, skip pure guesses)
  Numerical section — since there's NO penalty for a wrong
    numerical answer, attempting every numerical question you
    have ANY working method for is strictly better than leaving
    it blank, even with low confidence in the final answer
```
This is a genuinely important, JEE-Main-specific strategic distinction from exams with uniform negative marking across all question types — treating the numerical section with the same guess-conservatively logic that correctly applies to the MCQ section is a real, avoidable strategic mistake, since there's no downside risk to attempting a numerical question you have partial working for, only potential upside.

## NCERT as the actual foundation, not a "basic" step to rush past

```
A common preparation mistake: treating NCERT textbook content
  as something to complete quickly before "real" advanced
  problem-solving begins, rather than as genuinely foundational
  material worth mastering deeply

Reality: a substantial share of JEE Main questions (more than
  many candidates initially expect, especially in Chemistry
  and parts of Physics) are directly answerable from solid
  NCERT-level conceptual understanding, without requiring the
  more elaborate multi-concept synthesis that JEE Advanced
  questions demand
```
This connects directly to the Overview tab's JEE Main vs. JEE Advanced comparison — Main's more formula-direct question style means genuine NCERT mastery has disproportionately high value here specifically, compared to JEE Advanced where NCERT alone is insufficient preparation. Rushing through NCERT to reach "advanced" problem sets sooner, without genuine retention of the NCERT-level material, is a common way candidates leave real, accessible marks on the table in exactly the section of questions that should be their most reliable, highest-confidence points.

## Building genuine formula recall, not just recognition

```
Recognition (weaker): "I've seen this formula before, I think
  it's roughly this" — often correct on easy recall but breaks
  down under time pressure or when a formula needs to be
  derived/adapted slightly for a non-standard question variant

Genuine recall (stronger): being able to write the formula
  correctly from memory, understand where each term comes from,
  and derive a needed variant quickly if the exact memorized
  form doesn't directly apply
```
Building genuine recall — not just passive recognition — matters specifically because JEE Main's time pressure (90 questions in 180 minutes) doesn't allow time to slowly reconstruct a half-remembered formula during the exam itself; a handwritten, personally-maintained formula sheet per chapter (mentioned in this platform's NEET content as well, for Physics specifically) built and reviewed repeatedly during preparation is what converts passive recognition into fast, reliable recall available instantly under real exam time pressure.

## Treating the January attempt as a genuine attempt, not a practice run

The Overview tab's guidance to treat January as a real attempt, not just practice, deserves the fundamentals-level emphasis it gets: a candidate who mentally treats January as low-stakes practice often under-prepares for it specifically, then discovers in April that their preparation timeline was effectively compressed by treating the first of two genuine opportunities as disposable. Since your best score across both sessions counts toward JoSAA counselling, a strong January score removes real pressure from April (allowing April to genuinely be an improvement attempt rather than a must-succeed one) — but this benefit only materializes if January is approached with genuine preparation and effort, not deferred to April by treating January as a low-stakes trial run.
