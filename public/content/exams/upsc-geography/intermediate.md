# UPSC Geography Optional — Intermediate (Paper I: Principles of Geography)

Paper I has two sections of five units each: **Physical Geography** (Section A) and **Human Geography** (Section B). This is the exact structure and topic list as given in UPSC's official syllabus.

## Section A: Physical Geography

```
1. GEOMORPHOLOGY:
   Factors controlling landform development | Endogenetic and exogenetic forces
   Origin and evolution of the earth's crust | Fundamentals of geomagnetism
   Physical conditions of the earth's interior | Geosynclines | Continental drift
   Isostasy | Plate tectonics | Recent views on mountain building | Vulcanicity
   Earthquakes and Tsunamis | Concepts of geomorphic cycles and landscape development
   Denudation chronology | Channel morphology | Erosion surfaces | Slope development
   Applied Geomorphology: geohydrology, economic geology and environment

2. CLIMATOLOGY:
   Temperature and pressure belts of the world | Heat budget of the earth
   Atmospheric circulation | Atmospheric stability and instability
   Planetary and local winds | Monsoons and jet streams
   Air masses and frontogenesis | Temperate and tropical cyclones
   Types and distribution of precipitation | Weather and climate
   Koppen's, Thornthwaite's and Trewartha's classification of world climates
   Hydrological cycle | Global climatic change and role/response of man
   Applied climatology and urban climate

3. OCEANOGRAPHY:
   Bottom topography of the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific Oceans
   Temperature and salinity of the oceans | Heat and salt budgets | Ocean deposits
   Waves, currents and tides | Marine resources: biotic, mineral and energy resources
   Coral reefs, coral bleaching | Sea-level changes | Law of the sea and marine pollution

4. BIOGEOGRAPHY:
   Genesis of soils | Classification and distribution of soils | Soil profile
   Soil erosion, degradation, and conservation
   Factors influencing world distribution of plants and animals
   Problems of deforestation and conservation measures
   Social forestry, agro-forestry | Wildlife | Major gene pool centers

5. ENVIRONMENTAL GEOGRAPHY:
   Principle of ecology | Human ecological adaptations
   Influence of man on ecology and environment
   Global and regional ecological changes and imbalances
   Ecosystems: their management and conservation
   Environmental degradation, management, and conservation
   Biodiversity and sustainable development | Environmental policy
   Environmental hazards and remedial measures
   Environmental education and legislation
```

### How to actually study Section A

The five units are not independent silos — they build on each other in the order given. Geomorphology (landforms) sets the physical stage; Climatology (weather/climate patterns) explains why those landforms experience the rainfall/temperature regimes they do; Oceanography extends the same atmospheric principles to ocean behaviour; Biogeography (soils, flora, fauna) is a direct consequence of the climate and landform combination; Environmental Geography is the synthesis unit — how humans interact with everything covered in units 1-4. Studying them in sequence, rather than jumping around, makes each new unit easier because it reuses concepts from the one before it.

**High-yield linkage to memorise:** Plate tectonics (Geomorphology) → determines mountain location → mountains force orographic rainfall (Climatology) → rainfall pattern determines soil type and vegetation belt (Biogeography) → human settlement and land use responds to all of the above (sets up Human Geography and, later, Paper II).

## Section B: Human Geography

```
1. PERSPECTIVES IN HUMAN GEOGRAPHY:
   Areal differentiation | Regional synthesis | Dichotomy and dualism
   Environmentalism | Quantitative revolution and locational analysis
   Radical, behavioral, human and welfare approaches
   Languages, religions, and secularization | Cultural regions of the world
   Human Development Index

2. ECONOMIC GEOGRAPHY:
   World economic development: measurement and problems
   World resources and their distribution | Energy crisis | The limits to growth
   World agriculture: typology of agricultural regions
   Agricultural inputs and productivity | Food and nutrition problems
   Food security | Famine: causes, effects and remedies
   World industries: locational patterns and problems | Patterns of world trade

3. POPULATION AND SETTLEMENT GEOGRAPHY:
   Growth and distribution of world population | Demographic attributes

   Social well-being and quality of life | Population as social capital

   Concepts of primate city and rank-size rule | Functional classification of towns
   Sphere of urban influence | Rural-urban fringe | Satellite towns

   Concept of a region | Types of regions and methods of regionalization

   Systems analysis in Human Geography

   Central Place theories of Christaller and Losch

```

### How to actually study Section B

Unit 5 (Models, Theories and Laws) is the unit examiners return to most often precisely because it is name-and-mechanism testable — "Explain Von Thunen's model" is a cleaner, more objectively gradable question than an open-ended discussion. Learn every named model in Unit 5 with: (a) who proposed it, (b) what it explains, (c) one labelled diagram, (d) one real-world limitation. That four-part template turns a memorisation risk into a scoring strength.

Units 1-4 build the vocabulary and framework that Unit 5's models then formalise mathematically/diagrammatically — e.g., Unit 3's "hierarchy of urban settlements" is the descriptive version of Unit 5's Central Place Theory (Christaller); Unit 2's "typology of agricultural regions" is the descriptive version of Unit 5's Von Thunen model. Recognising these pairings cuts revision time significantly since you're really learning one idea twice, at two levels of formality.

## Cross-cutting exam skill: the map question habit starts now

Even though the compulsory map question belongs to Paper II, Paper I concepts (ocean currents, climate zones, plate boundaries, population distribution) are also frequently asked as sketch-map or diagram-supported answers. Build the habit here: every time you learn a Paper I concept that has a spatial dimension, sketch it — don't just read about it.

## Study resources
- **Savindra Singh** — Geomorphology, Climatology, Oceanography (Physical Geography section reference).
- **Majid Husain, "Geography of India"** and his **"Fundamentals of Human Geography"** (for models/theories, cited widely for optional-level depth).
- **R.D. Dikshit, "Geographical Thought"** — for Perspectives in Human Geography (Unit 1).
- **Previous Year Question papers (upsc.gov.in)** — the single most reliable guide to which sub-topics within each unit are actually tested most often.
