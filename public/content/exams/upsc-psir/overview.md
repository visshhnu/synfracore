# UPSC PSIR Optional — Overview

## What is the PSIR Optional?

Every UPSC Civil Services Mains candidate must pick one **optional subject**, examined across two papers (Optional Paper 1 and Optional Paper 2, 250 marks each — 500 marks total, out of the Mains' overall 1750 written marks). Political Science and International Relations (PSIR) is consistently one of the most chosen humanities optionals, both by political science graduates and by candidates from other backgrounds who find its subject matter directly useful for the General Studies papers.

Think of it this way: GS Paper 2 (Polity, Governance, International Relations) makes you a generalist who can answer a moderately deep question about the Indian Constitution or a UN body. The PSIR optional is where UPSC checks whether you can go **deep** on the same territory — trace an idea back to the political theorist who first argued it, compare how two different countries' political systems actually work, and analyse India's foreign policy choices with the vocabulary of International Relations theory rather than newspaper paraphrase.

## The hook: why candidates choose PSIR

Picture two candidates answering "Why does India oppose an unconditional Non-Aligned stance today the way it did in 1961?" One writes what they remember from a current-affairs magazine. The other frames the answer using the actual IR concepts — bipolarity, balance of power, strategic autonomy — situates it against the historical NAM phases, and closes with a reasoned position, the same way a GS4 Ethics answer rewards balanced judgment over a one-line verdict. The second candidate is not smarter; they have studied PSIR, which hands you the analytical vocabulary GS2 alone never fully builds. That overlap is the entire pitch: nearly **60-70% of PSIR's content directly reinforces GS Paper 2** (Polity + International Relations + Governance), so study time spent here pays out twice.

## Analogy: PSIR optional is a "vocabulary upgrade"

If GS2's polity and IR sections are like knowing enough of a foreign language to order food and ask directions, the PSIR optional is like studying that language's grammar and literature properly — suddenly you can read the newspaper editorial, not just the menu. A GS2 answer describing "India's foreign policy shift" in plain English becomes a PSIR-trained answer that names the shift as a move from Nehruvian non-alignment to multi-alignment, cites the relevant theoretical lens (realism vs. idealism), and grounds it with a historical marker (post-1991 liberalisation, or the 1998 nuclear tests). Same question, structurally deeper answer, because the optional gave you the terminology GS alone doesn't require.

Each paper follows UPSC's standard optional-paper question pattern: 8 questions across two sections (4 in Section A, 4 in Section B). Question 1 and Question 5 are compulsory; candidates attempt 5 questions total, with at least one from each section. Unlike Geography, PSIR carries **no compulsory map-based question** — every answer is discursive/analytical, which changes the preparation emphasis toward argument structure and theoretical framing rather than diagram/map work. Paper I builds the conceptual toolkit (what is the state, what is justice, how does Indian democracy actually function) and then Section B applies parts of that toolkit to India's own institutions. Paper II mirrors the same logic at the international scale: Section A gives you comparative-politics method and IR theory, and Section B is India's foreign policy read through that same theory. This is why most PSIR aspirants are advised to build Political Theory (Paper I, Section A) solidly first — Indian Politics, Comparative Politics, and IR all repeatedly borrow its vocabulary (legitimacy, ideology, the state, rights).

## Annotated example

**Question style:** "Critically examine the debate on the Basic Structure doctrine in the context of Parliament's amending power." (Paper I, Section B)

A strong answer:
1. **Introduction (1-2 lines):** State what the Basic Structure doctrine is and name the case that established it (*Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala*, 1973).
2. **Body — the underlying tension:** Parliament's constituent power to amend (Article 368) versus judicial review protecting core constitutional values — this is the same "power vs. legitimacy" tension studied abstractly in Political Theory (Section A), now applied concretely.
3. **Body — evolution:** Contrast the pre-Kesavananda position (*Golaknath*, treating Fundamental Rights as unamendable in a different way) with post-Kesavananda case law refining what counts as "basic" (federalism, secularism, judicial review itself, free and fair elections).
4. **Critical angle:** Present both sides — critics call it undemocratic judicial overreach limiting a sovereign Parliament; defenders call it the only safeguard against a majoritarian Parliament dismantling constitutionalism itself. This is the analytical layer that separates a good answer from a descriptive one.
5. **Conclusion:** A reasoned judgment, not a dodge — e.g., that the doctrine has held up as a stabilising check without freezing constitutional evolution, citing a recent example if one is current.

This define → contextualise → present both sides → judge pattern is the PSIR house style: theory and Indian polity are never asked as pure recall, they are asked as "critically examine" or "discuss," which always expects a stance.

## Try it (2 minutes)

Before reading further into this course, take two minutes and write, from memory, one sentence each defining these four words the way a political theorist would (not a dictionary): **state, sovereignty, power, justice**. Don't worry about getting them "textbook-perfect" yet — the point is to notice which of the four you can define crisply already and which feel fuzzy. You'll redo this same exercise after the Fundamentals section and compare.

## Who should pick this optional

- Candidates with a political science, public administration, law, or history background have a natural head start on the vocabulary and reading style.
- Strong performers in GS2 (Polity and IR) tend to adapt quickly, since the two subjects reinforce each other from day one.
- Candidates comfortable with argumentative, thesis-driven writing (rather than descriptive listing) score well — PSIR rewards a clear position defended with evidence, not encyclopaedic recall.
- Less ideal for candidates who strongly prefer numerical, diagram-heavy, or map-based subjects (contrast with Geography) — PSIR is almost entirely discursive prose.
