# UPSC PSIR Optional — Interview (DAF) Preparation

## How the optional subject shows up at the interview stage

The Personality Test (interview), worth 275 marks, is conducted by a UPSC board based heavily on your **Detailed Application Form (DAF)** — the form you fill in after clearing Mains, which explicitly asks for your optional subject. Declaring PSIR as your optional means the board can, and often does, probe it — not to re-test the syllabus the way Mains did, but to check whether you can hold a reasoned, current, and administratively relevant conversation about politics and international affairs without sounding like you're reciting a Mains answer.

## What examiners are actually testing (it's not a re-exam)

PSIR is arguably the optional most exposed to interview probing, because political theory and international relations are also simply what informed adults are expected to have opinions on. Boards typically probe three things through optional-subject questions:

1. **Genuineness and currency of interest** — did you pick PSIR because you actually follow politics and international affairs, or purely for GS2 overlap and scoring reasons? Questions like "what's a recent political development you've been following" test this directly, and a candidate who can only discuss syllabus theorists, not this week's news, reveals a scoring-only motivation.
2. **Ability to hold a balanced, non-partisan position** — since PSIR touches live political controversies (federalism disputes, party politics, foreign-policy choices), boards specifically test whether you can discuss these with analytical distance rather than a personal political stance, which is a core requirement for a future civil servant who must serve any elected government neutrally.
3. **Application to administration** — can you connect a political-science or IR concept to the job you're applying for? E.g., "How would understanding centre-state relations help you as a district officer implementing a centrally sponsored scheme?"

## Sample question types (with the reasoning behind each)

### Type 1: Concept-check, casually phrased
*"You've studied theories of the state — in your own words, what do you think the state's main job actually is today?"*
- **What's being tested:** Whether you can explain a technical theoretical distinction (Liberal vs. Marxist vs. Pluralist views of the state) in plain language and form your own reasoned synthesis, rather than just reciting definitions — a core administrative communication skill.

### Type 2: Neutrality stress-test
*"Do you personally think India's reservation policy has achieved its goals?"*
- **What's being tested:** This is deliberately a live political controversy. The board wants to see if you can present a Rawlsian justice-and-equality-informed, balanced analysis (citing both the affirmative-action rationale and legitimate critiques around implementation) without either dodging the question or taking an inflammatory personal stance — exactly the balanced-judgment instinct rewarded in this course's Overview annotated example and consistent with GS4 Ethics case-study expectations.

### Type 3: Current-affairs bridge
*"There's been a recent development in [a bilateral relationship or regional grouping]. As someone who studied International Relations, how would you explain its significance to a colleague outside the foreign service?"*
- **What's being tested:** Whether you can connect Paper II's theoretical vocabulary (balance of power, strategic autonomy, regionalisation) to a live event and explain it plainly — not whether you can cite the Mains syllabus's own (dated) illustrative examples flagged in this course's Advanced section.

### Type 4: Home-state/home-region politics
*"You're from [your state] — how would you describe centre-state relations from that state's perspective?"*
- **What's being tested:** DAF-driven — boards frequently ask candidates to apply their optional to their own declared home state, since this checks whether the knowledge is real and locally groundable, not abstract textbook recall.

### Type 5: Cross-subject bridge to your graduation background
*"Your degree is in [X] — how does that connect to your choice of PSIR as an optional?"*
- **What's being tested:** Coherence of your overall profile as stated in the DAF; a board is often gently checking whether your subject choices tell a consistent story, the same check made across every optional subject on this platform.

## How to prepare specifically for this

- **Re-read your own DAF** as if you were the interviewer — anticipate every politics/IR-adjacent question your own stated details (home state, graduation subject, hobbies if politics-adjacent) could invite.
- **Build a "current-affairs-meets-PSIR" running list** in the weeks before the interview — recent elections, Supreme Court judgments touching federalism or rights, foreign-policy summits or crises, UN reform developments — and practice connecting each to a specific Paper I or Paper II unit out loud, the same bridge-building habit developed in this course's Projects section.
- **Practice neutrality deliberately** — unlike a written Mains answer, an interview answer on a politically live topic is being watched for tone and balance as much as content. Practice stating "here is the strongest argument on each side, and here is where I land and why" out loud with a mock panel, not just in writing.
- **Know your own state/region's politics cold** — party landscape, a live centre-state issue if one exists, one recent development. This is asked often enough that it deserves dedicated preparation, not general revision.
- **Do not overclaim expertise** — if a question goes beyond what you genuinely know (a very specific bilateral treaty detail, an obscure theorist), it is better to say so honestly and reason through what you do know, than to bluff; boards are trained to probe further exactly when they sense a bluff, especially on a subject this current-affairs-adjacent.
