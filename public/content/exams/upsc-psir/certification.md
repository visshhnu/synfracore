# UPSC PSIR Optional — Scoring & What Separates Good from Average

There is no external "certification" for a UPSC optional subject — you don't get a credential for studying PSIR the way you would for a technical certification exam. What matters instead is exactly how UPSC scores this optional and what measurably different behaviour separates candidates who score well (typically 300+/500 combined) from those who score average-to-poor (typically low-to-mid 200s/500), even when both groups have read a similar volume of theory.

## How the optional is actually scored

- Two papers, 250 marks each, 500 marks total — folded directly into the overall Mains merit ranking alongside the four GS papers and the Essay paper.
- Each paper: 8 questions across two sections, 5 to be attempted, Question 1 and Question 5 compulsory, at least one attempt from each section.
- There is no negative marking on Mains descriptive papers — an incomplete or partially correct answer still earns partial credit, so leaving a question blank is close to the worst option, worse than attempting it imperfectly `(needs verification — recheck the exact current-year question format/compulsory-question rule against the latest official notification, since this detail can shift year to year independent of the syllabus)`.
- Examiners mark holistically against a model answer/scheme, with real weight given to argument structure, critical/analytical depth, and accurate use of theoretical vocabulary — this qualitative emphasis (not a hard numeric formula) is well-documented across successful candidates' post-result analyses and is consistent with this platform's `upsc-geography` technology's documented scoring behaviour for a different optional `(needs verification — the general shape of this is well established but exact marking-scheme weightage is not publicly quantified by UPSC and should be treated as informed pattern, not official rule)`.

## What separates a good scorer from an average one

### 1. A clear thesis, not a list of facts
Average scorers write everything they know about a topic in roughly chronological or textbook order. Good scorers open with a one-line position or framing, then organise the body around defending that position — because PSIR questions almost always carry an implicit "critically examine," "discuss," or "to what extent," and a list of facts without a governing argument reads as incomplete even when factually correct. This is the same define → contextualise → critique → judge structure demonstrated in this course's Overview and Advanced sections.

### 2. Named theoretical vocabulary used precisely, not decoratively
Average answers drop a thinker's name without using their actual framework ("as Rawls said, justice matters" adds nothing). Good scorers use the specific mechanism a theorist actually proposed (the veil of ignorance, the difference principle) to do real analytical work in the answer — explaining *why* a policy is just or unjust in that theorist's own terms, not just citing the name for credibility.

### 3. Both sides of a debate, genuinely represented
Because so much of PSIR (theories of state, justice, democracy, IR approaches) is inherently about competing schools of thought, average answers pick one side and ignore the other. Good scorers present the strongest version of the opposing view before explaining why their own position still holds — this is explicitly what "critically examine" is asking for, and its absence is one of the most common reasons a factually correct answer still scores modestly.

### 4. Current, India-grounded or currently-live examples over textbook-only ones
An IR concept explained purely in the abstract (balance of power described only in 19th-century European terms) scores lower than the same concept explained with a current application (balance of power dynamics in the Indo-Pacific today). This platform's Advanced section flags that the syllabus's own Section B "recent developments" examples are dated — good scorers actively replace them with genuinely current material, exactly the discipline built in this course's Projects section (current-affairs-to-theory bridge notes).

### 5. Time discipline across all five questions
Because there's no negative marking, a common average-scorer mistake is over-investing in the first two questions and rushing (or leaving incomplete) the last one. Good scorers allocate time strictly by marks (roughly 1 minute per mark) and protect enough time to attempt all five questions with at least a basic thesis-and-structure, rather than writing three excellent answers and one blank.

### 6. Command over both papers, not a lopsided one
Because Political Theory (Paper I, Section A) is the conceptual toolkit for everything else — Indian Politics, Comparative Politics, and IR all repeatedly borrow its vocabulary — candidates who rush through theory to "get to the interesting current-affairs parts" tend to write Paper II answers that state opinions without a theoretical anchor. Good scorers keep visibly reinforcing Section A concepts inside Paper I Section B and Paper II answers (e.g., citing the general theory-of-state framework while explaining a specific centre-state dispute), the same cross-referencing habit highlighted in the Intermediate section of this course.

## Realistic scoring expectations

Optional subject scores at UPSC Mains are famously compressed compared to what candidates expect from an academic background — a genuinely strong answer set might still land in the 130-150/250 per paper range rather than the 200+ a university exam might reward, because UPSC evaluators grade optionals on a comparative, ranked-cohort basis rather than an absolute correctness scale `(needs verification — recheck current-year cutoff/scoring trends against a recent official UPSC result analysis or the marks statement UPSC publishes post-result, since these patterns shift year to year and this platform's Volatile Core policy flags any marks/cutoff trend claim for revalidation)`.
