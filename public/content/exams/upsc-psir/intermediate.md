# UPSC PSIR Optional — Intermediate (Paper I: Political Theory and Indian Politics)

Paper I has two sections: **Political Theory** (Section A, 10 topic areas) and **Indian Government and Politics** (Section B, 11 topic areas). This is the exact structure and topic list as given in UPSC's official syllabus.

## Section A: Political Theory

```
1. POLITICAL THEORY: Meaning and approaches.

2. THEORIES OF STATE: Liberal, Neo-liberal, Marxist, Pluralist,
   post-colonial and Feminist.

3. JUSTICE: Conceptions of justice with special reference to Rawls'
   theory of justice and its communitarian critiques.

4. EQUALITY: Social, political and economic; relationship between
   equality and freedom; Affirmative action.

5. RIGHTS: Meaning and theories; different kinds of rights;
   concept of Human Rights.

6. DEMOCRACY: Classical and contemporary theories; different models
   of democracy — representative, participatory and deliberative.

7. CONCEPT OF POWER: Hegemony, ideology and legitimacy.

8. POLITICAL IDEOLOGIES: Liberalism, Socialism, Marxism, Fascism,
   Gandhism and Feminism.

9. INDIAN POLITICAL THOUGHT: Dharmashastra, Arthashastra and Buddhist
   traditions; Sir Syed Ahmed Khan, Sri Aurobindo, M. K. Gandhi,
   B. R. Ambedkar, M. N. Roy.

10. WESTERN POLITICAL THOUGHT: Plato, Aristotle, Machiavelli, Hobbes,
    Locke, John S. Mill, Marx, Gramsci, Hannah Arendt.
```

### How to actually study Section A

The ten topics are not independent silos — units 1-7 build the abstract vocabulary (what is the state, what is justice, what is power) and units 8-10 are where that vocabulary gets attached to actual names and movements. Studying units 1-7 first, thoroughly, makes units 8-10 dramatically faster, because "explain Gandhism as a political ideology" becomes an exercise in mapping Gandhi's ideas onto concepts you already know (power as non-violent moral force rather than coercion, a communitarian-adjacent view of justice) rather than learning an entirely new framework from scratch.

**High-yield linkage to memorise:** A theory of state (Unit 2) implies a view of power and legitimacy (Unit 7) implies a stance on what rights and equality should look like (Units 4-5) implies which ideology it lines up with (Unit 8). Marxist state theory → power as class domination → scepticism of purely formal/legal rights → Marxism as ideology. Learning this one chain well means four separate-looking syllabus units actually reinforce a single argument, which is exactly the kind of connected answer examiners reward over four disconnected definitions.

**Named thinkers deserve a fixed template.** For every thinker in Units 9-10, learn: (a) their core political idea in one sentence, (b) which broader theory/ideology they belong to or influenced, (c) one specific text or concept associated with them, (d) one standard criticism. This turns "discuss Hannah Arendt's concept of power" from an open-ended essay risk into a structured four-part answer.

## Section B: Indian Government and Politics

```
1. INDIAN NATIONALISM:
   (a) Political strategies of India's freedom struggle:
       constitutionalism to mass Satyagraha, Non-cooperation,
       Civil Disobedience; militant and revolutionary movements;
       peasant and workers' movements.
   (b) Perspectives on the Indian national movement: liberal,
       socialist and Marxist; radical humanist and Dalit.

2. MAKING OF THE INDIAN CONSTITUTION: Legacies of British rule;
   different social and political perspectives.

3. SALIENT FEATURES OF THE INDIAN CONSTITUTION: The Preamble,
   Fundamental Rights and Duties, Directive Principles; Parliamentary
   system and amendment procedures; judicial review and the Basic
   Structure doctrine.

4. PRINCIPAL ORGANS OF GOVERNMENT:
   (a) Union Government: envisaged role and actual working of the
       Executive, Legislature and Supreme Court.
   (b) State Government: envisaged role and actual working of the
       Executive, Legislature and High Courts.

5. GRASSROOTS DEMOCRACY: Panchayati Raj and Municipal Government;
   significance of the 73rd and 74th Amendments; grassroots movements.

6. STATUTORY INSTITUTIONS/COMMISSIONS: Election Commission,
   Comptroller and Auditor General, Finance Commission, Union Public
   Service Commission, National Commission for Scheduled Castes,
   National Commission for Scheduled Tribes, National Commission for
   Women, National Human Rights Commission, National Commission for
   Minorities, National Backward Classes Commission.

7. FEDERALISM: Constitutional provisions; changing nature of
   centre-state relations; integrationist tendencies and regional
   aspirations; inter-state disputes.

8. PLANNING AND ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT: Nehruvian and Gandhian
   perspectives; role of planning and the public sector; Green
   Revolution, land reforms and agrarian relations; liberalisation
   and economic reforms.

9. CASTE, RELIGION AND ETHNICITY IN INDIAN POLITICS.

10. PARTY SYSTEM: National and regional parties; ideological and
    social bases of parties; patterns of coalition politics; pressure
    groups; trends in electoral behaviour; changing socio-economic
    profile of legislators.

11. SOCIAL MOVEMENTS: Civil liberties and human rights movements;
    women's movements; environmentalist movements.
```

### How to actually study Section B

Units 2-4 (Constitution-making, salient features, organs of government) are the descriptive/institutional backbone — get these solid first, because units 7-11 are all really "stress tests" of that backbone under real political conditions (federalism stress-tests Union-State design, the party system stress-tests representative democracy, social movements stress-test how rights and civil liberties actually function in practice). A candidate who knows the Constitution's design well can improvise a reasonable answer on almost any Unit 7-11 topic even without having memorised every detail of that specific unit, because the underlying institutional logic carries over.

**Cross-reference Section A into every Section B answer.** Section B is explicitly the "Indian Politics" half of a paper whose first half is Political Theory — examiners consistently reward answers that connect the two, e.g., using Unit 2's (Section A) theories of state/power to explain *why* the Basic Structure doctrine emerged as a judicial check (Unit 3, Section B), rather than treating the two sections as unrelated. This same "reuse the abstract concept concretely" habit is emphasised in this course's Overview annotated example.

## Cross-cutting exam skill: the "critically examine" instinct starts now

PSIR questions are rarely pure "describe X." Most carry an implicit or explicit "critically examine," "discuss," "to what extent," or "in light of recent developments" framing. Build the habit here: for every Section A concept and every Section B institution, keep a running one-line note of at least one criticism or limitation alongside the standard description — this is the raw material every strong PSIR answer eventually needs.

## Study resources
- **O.P. Gauba, "An Introduction to Political Theory"** — Section A reference (Political Theory, ideologies, thinkers).
- **M. Laxmikanth, "Indian Polity"** — the standard reference for Section B's constitutional and institutional detail.
- **Rajeev Bhargava & Ashok Acharya (eds.), "Political Theory: An Introduction"** — supplementary for contemporary theoretical debates (Rawls, communitarianism, feminism, post-colonialism).
- **Previous Year Question papers (upsc.gov.in)** — the single most reliable guide to which sub-topics within each unit are actually tested most often.
