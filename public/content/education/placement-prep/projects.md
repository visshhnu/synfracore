# Placement Preparation — Practice Projects

Placement prep isn't a coding skill — it's a process, and the "projects" here are structured practice cycles for that process, not software builds. Each one is designed to produce a measurable artifact you can review and improve on, the same way you'd review code.

## Project 1: Resume Build-and-Audit Exercise (Beginner)

**Time:** 2-3 days

Build a real, one-page, ATS-optimized resume following this course's Overview guidance, then audit it against a concrete checklist rather than just "does it look nice."

### Steps

1. Draft a one-page resume: contact, education, skills, 2-3 projects (in STAR format — situation/task, action, measurable result), internships if any, achievements
2. Pick a real job description (any relevant listing) and check keyword alignment — does your resume use the same terms the JD uses for your actual skills?
3. Audit every project bullet: does it state a measurable result, or just describe what was built? ("Built full-stack e-commerce app" → does it say what scale, what stack, what specific improvement?)
4. Check the skills section against the "don't list" guidance — no soft skills like "good communication," no irrelevant tools
5. Get one outside review (a peer, a senior student, a mentor) and revise based on specific feedback, not just your own read

### What This Produces
- A real, usable, ATS-checked resume — not a practice artifact you throw away
- A documented before/after showing what specifically improved and why

### Artifact Name
`resume-audit-v1` (keep versions as you iterate — the improvement trail is itself useful to look back on)

## Project 2: Timed Aptitude/Reasoning Practice-Test Cycle (Intermediate)

**Time:** 2-3 weeks (ongoing cycle, not a single session)

Run a structured, repeatable practice cycle for the aptitude round specifically — take a timed mock test, score it by section, identify weak topics, drill them, then retest — tracking real improvement across cycles rather than one-off practice.

### Steps

1. Take one full timed mock aptitude test matching the real format (60-90 min, quantitative + logical reasoning + verbal sections)
2. Score it by section, not just overall — identify your 2-3 weakest specific topics (e.g., not just "quant" but specifically "time-speed-distance problems")
3. Drill those specific weak topics with focused practice sets for several days
4. Retake a fresh mock test (not the same one) and compare section-by-section scores against the first attempt
5. Repeat the cycle at least 3 times, and keep a log: date, overall score, section breakdown, and what you drilled in between

### What This Produces
- A documented score-improvement trend across multiple cycles, not just a single test result
- A clear, evidence-based picture of your actual weak areas — not a guess

### Artifact Name
`aptitude-practice-log`

## Project 3: Structured Mock-Interview Log — Technical and HR (Advanced)

**Time:** 2-3 weeks (ongoing)

Run and record at least 3 mock interviews — at minimum one technical/DSA round and one HR round — each followed by a structured self-review against this course's own "common mistakes" guidance, tracking specific improvement between attempts.

### Steps

1. Run a mock technical interview (with a peer, a mentor, or by timing yourself solving a problem while narrating out loud) — record it if possible
2. Immediately after, self-review against specific criteria: did you jump to code without clarifying constraints first? Did you state time/space complexity? Did you use meaningful variable names?
3. Run a mock HR round separately, covering at minimum "tell me about yourself," "why this company," and a weakness question — record your actual answers
4. Self-review the HR round against this course's guidance: was your "tell me about yourself" a 2-minute pitch or a rambling life story? Did your weakness answer show real self-awareness, or was it a cliché?
5. Repeat both types of mock interview at least 2 more times, and log specifically what changed between attempt 1 and attempt 3 — not just "I felt more confident," but concrete changes (e.g., "stopped jumping to code before clarifying constraints")

### What This Produces
- A documented interview-performance trend across multiple attempts, technical and HR both
- Specific, named improvements you can speak to if asked "how did you prepare"

### Artifact Name
`mock-interview-log`

## Tips for Great Practice Cycles

**Always produce a measurable artifact, not just "I practiced."** A score log, a before/after resume diff, a recorded mock interview with notes — these are what let you (and anyone reviewing your prep) actually see progress, the same way a commit history shows real work.

**Review yourself against specific criteria, not vague self-assessment.** "That went okay" is much weaker feedback than "I didn't ask about scale before designing, and I used single-letter variable names twice."

**Track trends across repeated cycles, not a single attempt.** One aptitude test score or one mock interview tells you almost nothing on its own — the pattern across 3+ attempts is what actually shows whether your prep is working.

**Match practice conditions to real conditions.** Timed aptitude tests should actually be timed. Mock interviews should actually be spoken out loud, not just thought through silently — the format mismatch between practice and the real thing is a common, avoidable gap.

## Practice Checklist
- [ ] A resume that's been audited against ATS/keyword criteria, not just visually reviewed
- [ ] At least 3 timed aptitude practice cycles with section-level score tracking
- [ ] At least 3 mock interviews (technical and HR) with structured self-review after each
- [ ] A documented improvement trend across attempts, not just a single data point
- [ ] Comfortable explaining, with specifics, how your preparation actually changed based on what you learned from practice
