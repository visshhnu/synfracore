# Daily Movement & Exercise — Intermediate

## Progressive overload: the actual mechanism behind getting stronger

Strength training only continues producing results if the body is progressively challenged beyond its current adapted capacity — this is progressive overload, and without it, a routine that once produced real gains plateaus, because the body has fully adapted to that specific stimulus:

```
Ways to progressively overload, roughly in order of how most
  beginner-to-intermediate programs apply them:
  1. Add weight/resistance (the most direct lever)
  2. Add reps at the same weight
  3. Add sets
  4. Improve range of motion / form quality at the same load
  5. Reduce rest time between sets (increases density)
```
The practical mistake worth avoiding: repeating the exact same workout (same exercises, same weight, same reps) indefinitely produces initial gains that then plateau, not because the exercises themselves stopped "working," but because the body has fully adapted and there's no longer a stimulus prompting further adaptation. Tracking workouts specifically (weight × reps × sets) — even simply, in a notes app — is what makes progressive overload actually deliberate and trackable, rather than assuming intensity is increasing when it's actually been static for weeks.

## HIIT vs. steady-state cardio: different tools, not competing philosophies

```
HIIT (High-Intensity Interval Training):
  Short bursts of near-maximal effort, alternated with recovery
  - Time-efficient — meaningful cardiovascular benefit in less
    total time than steady-state
  - Genuinely more taxing on recovery — not sustainable at
    high frequency without adequate rest
  - Requires higher baseline fitness to execute safely and
    effectively — not the ideal starting point for a true beginner

Steady-state (Zone 2, sustained moderate effort):
  - Lower recovery cost, sustainable at higher frequency
  - Builds aerobic base and mitochondrial density specifically
  - Takes longer per session for equivalent cardiovascular
    training stimulus
```
The practical guidance: HIIT is a genuine, evidence-supported tool for time-efficient cardiovascular training, but it's not a strict upgrade over steady-state — the two provide meaningfully different physiological adaptations (steady-state's role in building aerobic base and mitochondrial density specifically is not fully replaced by HIIT), and a program combining both, rather than exclusively one or the other, is generally better supported than an "HIIT is just more efficient so do only that" approach.

## Mobility work: the piece most programs skip until it becomes a problem

```
Mobility ≠ flexibility alone — mobility is active control through
  a joint's full range of motion, not just passive stretch length

Common gaps from prolonged sitting specifically:
  - Hip flexor tightness (hip flexors shortened from constant
    hip-flexed sitting position)
  - Thoracic spine stiffness (reduced rotation/extension from
    slouched desk posture)
  - Ankle dorsiflexion limitation (affects squat depth/form)
```
Mobility limitations from prolonged sitting don't just cause discomfort — they directly limit the quality and safety of strength training movements built on those same joints, most directly squat depth and form (limited ankle/hip mobility forces compensatory movement patterns that increase injury risk under load). This is the practical reason mobility work belongs alongside strength and cardio training, not as an optional add-on — someone with genuinely limited hip/ankle mobility attempting to load a squat pattern without addressing that mobility gap first is training around a limitation rather than resolving it, which tends to produce compensatory movement patterns that create real injury risk over time.

## Tracking that actually informs decisions, not just data collection

```
Genuinely useful tracking:
  - Workout log (weight/reps/sets) — makes progressive overload
    trackable and deliberate
  - Resting heart rate trend over weeks — a rising trend can
    signal overtraining, illness, or genuine improvement
    (declining trend), depending on direction and context
  - Steps/day trend — a rough NEAT proxy, useful for the
    sitting-pattern awareness covered on the Fundamentals tab

Data collected but rarely acted on:
  - Precise calorie-burn estimates from wearables (notoriously
    inaccurate, especially for strength training — useful as a
    rough relative trend, not an absolute number to rely on)
```
The practical filter worth applying to any tracking habit: does this specific metric actually change a decision (should I add weight this session, should I take a rest day, is my sitting pattern actually improving) — metrics that get logged but never actually inform a decision are tracking for its own sake, which is a real, common trap that consumes attention without producing proportional value.

## Balancing strength, cardio, and mobility across a week

```
A reasonable starting structure for someone building a
  well-rounded routine, not specialized for a specific sport:
  2-3 strength sessions/week (all major muscle groups covered
    across the week, not necessarily every session)
  2-3 cardio sessions/week (mix of zone 2 and occasional
    higher-intensity work)
  Daily mobility (even 5-10 minutes) — the highest-frequency,
    lowest-time-cost category, specifically because mobility
    gains are more responsive to frequency than to session length
```
The specific ratio matters less than genuine coverage across all three categories — a routine that's exclusively strength training, or exclusively cardio, each leaves a real gap (cardiovascular health, or muscular strength/bone density, respectively) that the excluded category specifically addresses — general long-term health, as distinct from training for a specific narrow goal, benefits from covering all three rather than optimizing one at the complete exclusion of the others.
