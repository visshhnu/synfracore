# Daily Movement & Exercise — Fundamentals

## NEAT: the movement that happens outside a workout entirely

Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis (NEAT) — the energy expended through all daily movement that isn't formal exercise (standing, walking to a meeting, fidgeting, taking stairs) — is a genuinely significant, often underestimated component of total daily activity, and it's the direct mechanism behind the Overview tab's finding that 8+ hours of sitting is an independent mortality risk factor even alongside separate formal exercise:

```
A person who exercises 1 hour daily but sits the remaining
  15-16 waking hours has dramatically lower total NEAT than
  someone who exercises 0 formal minutes but moves constantly
  throughout the day (standing desk, walking meetings, taking
  stairs, pacing during calls)

Both patterns can produce similar total calories burned, but
  the health outcomes associated with prolonged uninterrupted
  sitting are genuinely different from outcomes with frequent
  movement breaks — this is why "I already work out" doesn't
  fully offset "I sit for 10 hours straight" the way total
  calorie math alone would suggest
```
The practical implication worth internalizing at the fundamentals level: a single daily workout, however well-executed, is not a complete substitute for breaking up prolonged sitting throughout the day — both matter, largely independently, which is exactly why the Overview tab's 20-20-20-2 rule targets the sitting pattern specifically, separate from and in addition to any formal exercise routine.

## Reading your own heart rate zones, practically

```
Rough max heart rate estimate: 220 - age
  (a real, individual measurement is more accurate, but this
   estimate is a reasonable practical starting point)

Zone 1-2 (50-70% max HR)  — easy, conversational pace — walking,
  light cycling; this is where most "moderate aerobic" WHO
  guideline minutes should come from
Zone 3-4 (70-85% max HR)  — harder effort, breathing labored but
  sustainable — jogging, faster cycling
Zone 5 (85%+ max HR)      — near-maximal effort, sustainable only
  briefly — HIIT intervals
```
Most people significantly overestimate how hard "moderate" intensity should actually feel — a genuinely moderate-intensity walk should allow full conversation without gasping; if that's not possible, the pace is likely already in zone 3+ territory, which is a different, higher-intensity category than what "150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity" is specifically asking for. Understanding your own approximate zones (even from the rough age-based formula, without a dedicated heart rate monitor) helps calibrate whether a given activity is actually hitting the intensity level a specific goal requires.

## Building the habit: starting smaller than feels necessary

```
Common failure pattern: committing to an ambitious new routine
  (5 days/week, 45 minutes, immediately) → missing a day →
  feeling like the whole effort has failed → stopping entirely

More durable pattern: starting with a genuinely small,
  almost-too-easy commitment (10 minutes, 3 days/week) →
  building consistency FIRST → increasing duration/frequency
  only once the smaller habit feels automatic, not effortful
```
The psychological mechanism worth understanding: a habit that feels achievable even on a bad day (low motivation, busy schedule) is one that survives long enough to become genuinely automatic — an ambitious routine that only works on ideal days breaks the very first time life gets in the way, and one broken day often cascades into abandoning the whole effort. Starting smaller than feels necessary, and only scaling up once consistency is genuinely established, is a real, evidence-informed behavior-change strategy, not just a conservative suggestion for people who lack discipline.

## The bodyweight foundation: why these four movements specifically

```
Push-ups   — upper body pushing (chest, shoulders, triceps)
Squats     — lower body, the single most functional movement
             pattern (sitting/standing, stairs, most daily activity)
Plank      — core stability, distinct from ab-specific "crunching"
             movements — trains the core's actual functional role
             (resisting movement, not just producing it)
Glute bridges — posterior chain (glutes, hamstrings) — the muscle
             group most weakened by prolonged sitting specifically
```
These four movements, covered in the Overview tab's 20-minute workout, aren't an arbitrary selection — together they cover every major movement pattern (push, squat, core stability, hip hinge/posterior chain) using bodyweight alone, which is exactly why this specific combination is a genuinely sound foundation for someone starting from zero, before any equipment or more specialized programming becomes necessary or useful.

## Recognizing your own signals that more movement is needed

The Overview tab's "signs you need to move more" list is worth treating as an actual, actionable diagnostic checklist rather than a passive list to read once — afternoon energy crashes, difficulty concentrating after an hour at a desk, and a resting heart rate above 75 bpm are all measurable, checkable signals, not vague feelings. Tracking whether these signals improve after 2-3 weeks of consistently implementing the habits above (the 20-20-20-2 rule, a short daily walk) turns an abstract "movement is good for you" into personally-verified feedback specific to your own body's response — the same pattern-recognition principle covered for sleep tracking elsewhere in this academy's content.
