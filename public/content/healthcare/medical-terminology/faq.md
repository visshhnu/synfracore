# Medical Terminology — FAQ

**Do I need to memorize thousands of medical terms to succeed as a coder?**
No — the Overview tab's guidance is the practical reality: memorizing roughly 50 common roots, 20 prefixes, and 20 suffixes lets you decode the vast majority of terms encountered in real clinical documentation, including many you've never explicitly studied before. The word-building method is the durable skill; memorizing whole words is a much less efficient and less transferable approach.

**Why is medical terminology taught before ICD-10-CM coding itself?**
Because every code, and every physician's note describing what a code should represent, is built from medical terminology — attempting to learn coding guidelines before you can decode the underlying clinical vocabulary is like trying to read before learning the alphabet. You can memorize specific codes for specific terms you've studied, but you won't be able to correctly interpret and code a genuinely unfamiliar term without the underlying word-building skill.

**Are all medical abbreviations safe to use and interpret confidently?**
No — some abbreviations are genuinely ambiguous or have caused real, documented clinical errors from misinterpretation (a well-known example: "MS" can mean either multiple sclerosis or morphine sulfate). Healthcare accreditation bodies maintain "do not use" abbreviation lists specifically because of this risk. As a coder, encountering a genuinely ambiguous abbreviation in documentation is a legitimate reason to seek clarification rather than assume a meaning.

**If I can decode a term's word parts, do I always know exactly what it means clinically?**
Not always — a small number of word parts (most notably "myel/o," which can mean spinal cord or bone marrow) are genuinely ambiguous and require clinical context to interpret correctly, and eponyms (Parkinson's, Crohn's, Alzheimer's) carry no decodable structure at all. Mechanical word-part decoding gets you most of the way for the vast majority of terms, but real fluency also means recognizing these specific exceptions rather than applying the general method blindly to every term.

**Does medical terminology change over time, or is it a fixed body of knowledge I learn once?**
It evolves — some terms fall out of clinical use or get replaced with more precise or less stigmatizing language over time (for example, "mental retardation" has been replaced with "intellectual disability" in current clinical and coding usage). A coder working across documentation from different time periods needs enough awareness to recognize older terminology's current equivalent, and staying current with these shifts is an ongoing part of the profession, not a one-time learning task completed during initial training.

**How does terminology knowledge actually help me write a better physician query, not just decode a term?**
Genuine terminology fluency lets you identify precisely what information is actually missing from ambiguous documentation and ask for exactly that, rather than a vague general request for clarification. A query like "does 'nephropathy' here mean diabetic, hypertensive, or another specific etiology" demonstrates you understand exactly what's ambiguous (the general term doesn't specify cause, and cause determines the correct code) — this kind of precise query typically resolves faster and creates less friction with clinical staff than a vague one.
