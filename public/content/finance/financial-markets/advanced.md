# Financial Markets — Advanced

## Derivatives — instruments deriving value from an underlying asset

A **derivative** is a financial contract whose value is derived from the price of an underlying asset (a stock, index, commodity, or currency) rather than having independent value of its own. The two most common derivative types are **futures** (a standardized contract obligating both parties to buy/sell the underlying asset at a predetermined price on a specified future date) and **options** (a contract giving the buyer the *right but not the obligation* to buy or sell the underlying asset at a predetermined price within a specified period, in exchange for paying a premium to the option seller). This right-versus-obligation distinction between options and futures is the single most important conceptual difference between the two instrument types.

## Why derivatives exist — hedging and speculation

Derivatives serve two genuinely distinct purposes that are easy to conflate: **hedging** (using a derivative to reduce or offset an existing risk exposure — for instance, a company with future foreign-currency payment obligations might use currency derivatives to lock in an exchange rate, reducing uncertainty about the actual rupee cost) and **speculation** (using a derivative to take on new risk exposure in pursuit of profit from anticipated price movements, without an underlying position being hedged). The same derivative instrument can serve either purpose depending on who's using it and why — a futures contract used by a farmer to lock in a crop's future sale price is hedging, while the same type of contract used by a trader with no underlying crop position, betting purely on price movement, is speculation.

## Options pricing — the basic intuition

An option's premium (the price paid for the option itself) reflects several factors: the underlying asset's current price relative to the option's **strike price** (the predetermined transaction price), the time remaining until the option expires (more time generally means more premium, since there's more opportunity for the underlying price to move favorably), and the underlying asset's **volatility** (higher volatility generally means higher premium, since greater price-movement potential increases the chance the option becomes valuable). ## Leverage in derivatives — amplified risk and return

A defining feature of many derivative instruments is **leverage** — controlling a large notional position in the underlying asset while committing a much smaller amount of capital upfront (a margin, for futures, or a premium, for options), meaning both potential gains and potential losses are amplified relative to the capital actually committed. This leverage is precisely why derivatives, while genuinely useful for hedging (this file), also carry meaningfully higher risk than direct equity or debt investment when used speculatively — a relatively small adverse price movement in the underlying asset can produce a proportionally much larger loss relative to the capital committed, a risk profile fundamentally different from simply holding the underlying asset directly.
