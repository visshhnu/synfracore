# Law & Compliance — Intermediate

## Sending a legal notice: what it actually does and doesn't do

A legal notice is a formal written communication, typically drafted by an advocate, stating a grievance and demanding specific action within a stated deadline before further legal proceedings begin. It is **not** a court order and carries no independent enforcement power — its real function is to put the recipient on formal record as having been warned, which matters later if the dispute does end up in court (it demonstrates you attempted resolution first) and often triggers a settlement precisely because a formal notice signals genuine intent to pursue the matter, not just a complaint. The practical value is disproportionate to its low cost: many disputes (a builder withholding a security deposit, an employer withholding final settlement) resolve at this stage without ever reaching a courtroom, because responding seriously is cheaper for the other side than actually litigating.

## Consumer disputes: which forum, and why it matters

India's Consumer Protection Act 2019 created a three-tier system, and picking the right tier by claim value is the first practical decision:

| Forum | Claim value | Typical timeline |
|---|---|---|
| District Consumer Commission | Up to ₹1 crore | Months, not years — designed to be faster than civil court |
| State Consumer Commission | ₹1 crore – ₹10 crore | Appeals from District, or first filing above ₹1 crore |
| National Consumer Commission | Above ₹10 crore | Appeals from State, or first filing above ₹10 crore |

Consumer forums are deliberately built to be faster and less formal than regular civil courts — you can file without a lawyer, filing fees are low, and the process is designed around genuine consumer complaints (defective goods, deficient service, unfair trade practice), not complex commercial litigation. The e-Daakhil portal allows filing consumer complaints online without physically visiting the forum, which matters in practice since travel/logistics is a real reason people don't pursue legitimate small claims.

## Employment disputes: what actually has legal teeth

Not every workplace grievance has an equally strong legal remedy — knowing which ones do changes how you approach a dispute:

- **Unpaid wages/final settlement** — a genuinely strong claim; the Payment of Wages Act and state Shops & Establishments Acts create clear obligations, and a well-drafted notice to the employer's registered office, copying HR, is often enough on its own to trigger payment
- **Wrongful termination** — strength depends heavily on whether you're covered by the Industrial Disputes Act (workman classification matters here) versus a pure at-will private-sector role — this is genuinely fact-specific and worth an actual consultation rather than assuming either way
- **POSH complaints** — every organization with 10+ employees is legally required to have an Internal Complaints Committee; filing there starts a defined statutory process with timelines the company must follow, and failure to have a functioning ICC is itself a compliance violation you can escalate on
- **Non-compete enforcement against you** — Indian courts have consistently held post-employment non-competes largely unenforceable as a restraint of trade (Section 27, Indian Contract Act) — this is one of the more reliably favorable positions for an employee to be in, though NDAs covering genuine confidential information remain separately enforceable

## RTI: getting information out of a government body

The Right to Information Act gives every citizen a legal right to request information from public authorities, with the authority legally obligated to respond within 30 days (48 hours for matters concerning life or liberty). Filing is genuinely simple — a plain-language application, a nominal fee (typically ₹10), addressed to the Public Information Officer of the specific department. The value in practice: it converts an informal, ignorable request ("why hasn't my pension been processed") into one with a hard legal response deadline and an appeals process (First Appellate Authority, then the Information Commission) if ignored — the deadline itself is often what produces a response that months of informal follow-up didn't.

## Reviewing a contract before you sign it — the checklist that actually matters

Most individuals sign rental agreements, freelance contracts, and offer letters without reading past the compensation figure. The clauses that actually cause disputes later are elsewhere:

- **Termination/exit clause** — what notice period applies, on both sides, and what happens to any deposit or pending payment on exit
- **Liability and indemnity** — in a freelance/consulting contract, does it make you liable for the client's own downstream losses, disproportionate to what you were paid
- **Dispute resolution clause** — arbitration versus court, and critically *which city's jurisdiction* — a contract naming a court far from you meaningfully raises the practical cost of ever enforcing your own rights under it
- **IP assignment** — in an employment or freelance contract, what work product is explicitly assigned to the other party, and does that scope extend beyond what's reasonable (some contracts overreach into pre-existing or unrelated personal work)
- **Auto-renewal terms** — a clause that silently renews a contract unless you cancel within a narrow window before expiry is a common, deliberately easy-to-miss trap in service agreements

## GST basics for freelancers and small entrepreneurs

GST registration becomes mandatory once turnover crosses ₹20 lakh (₹10 lakh in special-category states) for services, or earlier if operating across state lines. Below that threshold, registration is optional but sometimes still worth doing voluntarily — many corporate clients require a GSTIN before they'll onboard a freelancer/vendor at all, regardless of your actual turnover. Once registered, GST compliance means monthly/quarterly return filing (GSTR-1 for outward supplies, GSTR-3B as the summary return) even in months with no invoices — the recurring administrative burden, not the tax rate itself, is usually the part that catches new freelancers off guard, and is worth budgeting time (or a part-time accountant) for from day one rather than after the first missed filing penalty.

## Reporting cyber fraud: the process, not just the number

Beyond the fundamentals-level advice to call the 1930 helpline immediately for active financial fraud, the follow-through matters for actual recovery: file a formal complaint at cybercrime.gov.in with all transaction references, screenshots, and communication records attached at the time of filing, not added later — the portal generates an acknowledgment number that both your bank and, if needed, the police cybercrime cell will reference. Banks are obligated under RBI's limited-liability framework to investigate promptly-reported unauthorized transactions, and a documented cybercrime.gov.in complaint is typically what your bank's own dispute process requires as supporting evidence — the two channels (bank dispute, cybercrime portal) work together, not as alternatives to each other.
